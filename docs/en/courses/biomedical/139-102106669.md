### Read the course as one signal chain

The clearest spine is `measurand → transducer → analog front end → sampling/filtering → display or control`. For ECG, an electrode turns a physiological potential into a microvolt differential signal; input impedance, CMRR, noise, and saturation determine whether the AFE preserves it; motion, mains interference, and a ground loop explain how contamination enters the record. Moving the same questions to pressure, flow, EMG, or pulse oximetry separates general measurement ideas from sensor-specific ones.

The later lectures name ventilators, pacemakers, defibrillators, and dialysis systems. Independent study here focuses on function blocks, signal paths, and failure modes; clinical operation lies outside the scope. Sensor drift can move a displayed value slowly, amplifier saturation can flatten an abnormal waveform, and a filter can remove a genuine low-amplitude structure. Those concrete paths teach more engineering judgment than memorizing a device list.

### A suitable computational exercise

The public page exposes no hardware lab, BOM, code repository, or weekly assignment specification. Use the PhysioNet [MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database](https://physionet.org/content/mitdb/1.0.0/) or a synthetic waveform for a non-diagnostic filtering exercise: retain the original segment, plot the filter response, and compare amplitude, delay, and edge effects before and after processing. Dataset labels may locate a segment; they do not justify a new medical interpretation.

For a deeper course in filtering, estimation, and image processing, MIT [HST.582J Biomedical Signal and Image Processing](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/hst-582j-biomedical-signal-and-image-processing-spring-2007/) provides computational assignments and labs. It is a follow-on computational course with a different laboratory scope from this NPTEL offering.

### Body-connected and clinical boundary

!!! danger "Do not connect a teaching circuit to a person"
    This page assumes no new human-data collection and no homemade electrode or circuit connected to a person. IEC 60601 and FDA appear in the syllabus because safety and regulation are discussed; that does not certify a device, confer clinical authority, or permit diagnosis. Patient connection, isolation, leakage, single-fault behavior, and compliance require current standards, qualified equipment, and a competent team.
