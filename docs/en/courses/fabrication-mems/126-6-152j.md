## The Same Course Is Different On and Off Campus

MIT 6.152J combines diffusion, oxidation, lithography, CVD, PVD, etching,
metallization, vacuum systems, CMOS, and advanced silicon processing with a
four-hour laboratory each week for 9 weeks. On campus, students fabricate
and test a MOS capacitor, microcantilever, and microfluidic device. For them,
the course joins process theory, cleanroom fabrication, testing, and reporting.
For an outside learner, only theory, process planning, data interpretation, and
report work are directly reproducible.

The official
[syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/pages/syllabus/)
lists “instructor permission,” not “no prerequisites.” For independent study,
check whether you can read pn/MOS cross sections and basic band diagrams, use
simple diffusion and reaction-rate equations, track material changes through
deposition, lithography, and etch, and write a technical report with units,
figures, and uncertainty.

## Use One Process Flow to Connect the Theory

The [calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/pages/calendar/)
contains too many topics to learn as isolated vocabulary. Maintain one process
flow and ask five questions:

1. **Change material:** what do oxidation, diffusion, and annealing change, and
   which parameters control the rate?
2. **Add a film:** how do CVD, PVD, sputtering, and evaporation affect coverage,
   stress, contamination, and thermal budget?
3. **Define a pattern:** how do resist, exposure, development, and alignment
   transfer layout into material?
4. **Remove material:** what do selectivity, anisotropy, and undercut mean for
   wet, dry, and KOH etches?
5. **Form a device:** how do those steps become MOS, MEMS, or microfluidic
   structures, and what is measured after each step?

The main material is the
[lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/resources/lecture-notes/);
there is no lecture video. Update the same cross section after every topic with
material, geometry, thermal budget, measurement, and failure risk instead of
collecting disconnected PDFs. The result should be one evolving fabrication
story, not a folder of files.

## PS1–8 Have Answers; the Whole Semester Does Not

The syllabus describes 10 homework assignments. The first 8 cover course
content; the last 2 are based on attending later lectures and asking
questions. Teams of up to four submit one solution.
[Assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/resources/assignments/)
publishes PS1–PS8 with solutions, but the final two activities are not
downloadable problem sets. “All ten homeworks are public” would therefore be
incorrect.

Assessment includes two 1.5-hour in-class quizzes and one take-home quiz.
[Exams](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/resources/exams/)
offers the take-home prompt, a Quiz 1 solution, and Quiz 2 examples or solutions
from other terms, not a one-to-one Fall 2005 set. The syllabus prose also says
lecture and lab carry equal weight while its listed components total 55%
non-lab and 45% lab. This is an inconsistency within the official page; the
guide preserves both statements instead of deciding which weighting governed
the course.

## Why All Three Laboratory Sequences End in Reports

The [Labs](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/pages/labs/)
page publishes report requirements, not a complete outside-user safety manual.
The calendar gives three sequences:

- **IC:** Gate Oxide/Poly → Backside Etch → Test, ending with a MOS capacitor;
- **MEMS:** Photolith → KOH Etch → Test, producing a microcantilever;
- **Fluids:** Photolith → Molding → Test, forming a microfluidic device.

The first IC report is graded for writing, returned, and revised before
technical and writing review. The MEMS report receives both technical and
writing review; the fluids report is graded only for technical content. The learning
outcome is not merely a fabricated object but an auditable account of process,
data, failure, and conclusion.

## How Far to Go without a Cleanroom

An outside learner can preserve that reasoning by drawing a process flow for
one device, marking material change, key parameter, contamination/alignment/
etch failure, and expected measurement at each step. A parameter sweep or
public dataset can then support a “pre-fabrication report” using the official
report questions. That practices process reasoning and technical writing; it
does not count as completing the MIT cleanroom laboratory.

## What Not to Do at Home

The original work depends on MIT ICL/TRL cleanrooms, an MTL identity card, and
course staff. Rules prohibit personal items, shorts, skirts, bare feet, and
exposed legs or other skin, in addition to facility procedures. Without institutional
access, equipment training, chemical and waste handling, on-site supervision,
and emergency procedures, do not turn resist, developer, strong acids or
bases, solvents, vacuum, high temperature, plasma, or KOH etching into a home
experiment.

The [course home](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/)
is from 2005. Fundamentals of diffusion, oxidation, deposition, lithography,
and etching remain a useful framework, but specific recipes, equipment, safety
rules, and “advanced” processes are dated. I would use it for process
fundamentals and report practice, not as a 2026 fab manual. Without a cleanroom,
study the theory, solve the problem sets, draw flows, and analyze data. Leave
physical fabrication to a qualified facility with training and on-site
supervision.
