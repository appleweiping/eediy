## The controller has to use the dynamics, not hide them

MIT 6.832 Underactuated Robotics is not a survey organized by robot type, nor an integration course that introduces perception, calls a planning library, and finally makes a manipulator move. The Spring 2022 course sets a sharper problem: systems may have fewer independent actuators than degrees of freedom, be constrained by contact or dynamics, and resist any attempt to hide the model behind joint servos. Pendulums, acrobots, quadrotors, and legged robots are vehicles for three recurring questions: how the system moves naturally, what limited control authority can change, and what inspectable evidence supports the claimed closed-loop behavior.

It was a graduate course. The Spring 2022 course information lists basic linear algebra and differential equations under prerequisites, then separately says that coding assignments will be in Python; the [Spring 2022 FAQ](https://underactuated.mit.edu/Spring2022/faqs.html) says that prior robotics is not required. Keep academic preparation separate from implementation language. If matrix dimensions, eigenvalues, gradients, or ODE integration are shaky, dynamic programming, Lyapunov analysis, and trajectory optimization will all suffer; weak Python or NumPy skills will instead block the notebooks. This is not a hardware course. A physical robot may appear in a chosen project, but it is not required for the public spine.

## Entry diagnostic: test models, numerics, and arguments first

Before Lecture 1, attempt three short tasks that do not depend on course answers:

1. Derive a state equation for a pendulum with mass, length, gravity, and damping. Label every unit, simulate one initial condition with two step sizes, and compare energy drift.
2. Plot a phase portrait for a two-dimensional nonlinear system, locate its equilibria, and explain the neighborhood in which its linearization is credible. Calling a plotting function without explaining the arrows and trajectories does not pass.
3. Write a small Python test that checks output shape, finite values, a dynamics residual, and one physical invariant. Replace “the plot looks right” with assertions another machine can repeat.

If the first task fails, revisit Lagrange/Newton modeling and ODEs. If the second fails, revisit linear systems, eigenvalues, and phase-plane reasoning. If the third fails, review NumPy, functional interfaces, deterministic seeds, and basic testing. Probability, convex optimization, and introductory control help, but waiting to finish all of them is unnecessary. Maintain a `prerequisite-ledger.md` instead: when the course first invokes a Bellman equation, semidefinite program, LQR, or expectation, record the review resource, a solved check problem, and any assumption that remains open.

## Twenty-five lectures form an evidence chain, not a topic list

The [official schedule](https://underactuated.mit.edu/Spring2022/schedule.html) lists 25 lectures. Independent study should not compress them into three labels—control, optimization, and planning—because the course’s value lies in testing the same closed-loop objective with successively stronger tools.

### Lectures 1–10: from dynamics to value functions

The first two lectures establish model-based control and nonlinear dynamics. Lectures 3–4 introduce dynamic programming; Lecture 5 exposes the structure of acrobots, cart-pole, and quadrotors; Lecture 6 advances dynamic programming again. Lectures 7–10 turn to Lyapunov methods and sum-of-squares. By the end of this segment, produce at least three kinds of evidence: a dynamics model card with coordinate conventions and units, a figure that places simulation beside an energy or residual check, and a one-page memo separating proved relationships among value functions, Lyapunov functions, and optimal cost from conjectured ones.

Do not treat a solver’s `success` flag as a stability proof. Whenever numerical search constructs a candidate function, preserve the problem statement, sampled or symbolic constraints, solver status, tolerances, and counterexample search. A check on a finite grid is empirical evidence; “no violation found on this grid” must not be promoted to a global claim.

### Lectures 11–18: trajectories, stabilization, and contact

Lectures 11–12 cover trajectory optimization, Lecture 13 trajectory stabilization, and Lecture 14 policy search. Lectures 15–18 proceed through simple walking, hybrid trajectory optimization, contact planning and control, and humanoids. This segment readily produces beautiful but unauditable animations. Attach the initial and terminal states, state and input bounds, time discretization, objective terms, dynamics defects, contact modes or complementarity conditions, and a perturbed closed-loop replay to every result.

For walking and contact, also draw an event timeline: identify intervals governed by continuous dynamics, impacts or mode changes that reset the state, and the side of each event on which the controller reads state. If a notebook hides those transitions, write a minimal hybrid example that exposes its guard, reset map, and failing initial conditions. That separates understanding hybrid dynamics from successfully replaying a demonstration.

### Lectures 19–25: planning, uncertainty, and output feedback

According to the official schedule above, Lectures 19–20 address motion planning and search, Lecture 21 stochastic dynamics, Lecture 22 robust control, Lecture 23 output feedback, Lecture 24 feedback motion planning, and Lecture 25 project presentations. This final segment is not a new subject. It asks how much of the earlier reasoning survives noise, model error, partial observation, and dependence on a single nominal trajectory.

Its central deliverable is an assumption ledger: whether state is measured, the noise distribution is known, model error is bounded, the planner is complete, and the controller has a certified region. Every result should move at least one entry from implicit to explicit. At the end of the course, this ledger reveals more mastery than a list of algorithm names.

## Seven assignments must connect derivation, code, and tests

The [assignment index](https://underactuated.mit.edu/Spring2022/assignments.html) exposes seven equally weighted assignments, together worth 35% in the original course. Each combines handwritten work with Python notebooks. Their sequence is Nonlinear Dynamics; Dynamic Programming; DP/Acrobots; Continuous-Time Value Iteration; Lyapunov; Trajectory Optimization; and Legged Robots. Public filenames include `vibrating_pendulum.ipynb`, `hopfield_network.ipynb`, `minimum_time.ipynb`, `lp_dp.ipynb`, `cartpole_balancing.ipynb`, `pendulum_cvi.ipynb`, `van_der_pol.ipynb`, `ilqr_driving.ipynb`, `orbital_transfer.ipynb`, `footstep_planning.ipynb`, and `compass_gait_limit_cycle.ipynb`.

Use a four-part record for every problem. First derive the result without inspecting a reference implementation. Next map every equation to a function, array dimension, and unit in the code. Then add limiting cases, randomized initial conditions, and residual tests. Finally write a correction memo classifying the first error as modeling, mathematics, numerics, or interface. Figures supplement tests; they do not replace quantitative checks.

The original assignment page directs enrolled students to work in Deepnote and submit through Gradescope, and it describes automated checks followed by staff review. Release dates, due dates, and the general late policy are public. An ordinary visitor lacks personal submission and late-status records, Gradescope scores, hidden or manual test results, staff feedback, and Piazza access. An independent learner can work through the public prompts and run self-authored tests but cannot call that an official grader pass. The [course-information page](https://underactuated.mit.edu/Spring2022/index.html) gives the remaining grade weights as a 30% midterm and a 35% final project, with no final exam; a public reproduction receives none of those course grades.

Create an `environment-lock.md` before starting. Record the operating system, Python, Drake and other package versions, selected repository commit, original notebook path, and local changes; save one clean execution log per assignment. If an old notebook fails against a current Drake API, preserve the original error and version information, then make the smallest migration and distinguish compatibility edits from solution code in the diff. Containers, lock files, regression tests, and commit records are EEDIY reproducibility supplements, not MIT deliverables, and they cannot reconstruct an unavailable 2022 grader.

The main computational route requires no robot purchase. Consider hardware only when a chosen project genuinely needs it and an appropriate laboratory, supervision, and equipment-safety process already exist. Otherwise, simulation, recorded data, or public models support a more faithful control argument. Do not add high-power actuators, flying vehicles, or human-carrying equipment merely to make a project look more robotic.

## Capstone: narrow one problem over four to five weeks

The [project specification](https://underactuated.mit.edu/Spring2022/project.html) allows an individual project and welcomes small teams of two or three. The work spans roughly four to five weeks and must address a dynamical system with an algorithmic analysis or control problem. Novel research is not required; reproducing a published result is acceptable, and Drake is not mandatory. Original deliverables included an approximately one-page proposal capped at 500 words, a one-paragraph progress update, a three-minute individual or five-minute team video, and an IEEE-style report. The page imposes no report-length requirement; it gives three to eight pages with figures and a few citations only as a reasonable reference. It asks for an abstract, motivation, related work when relevant, results and discussion, team contributions, and disclosure of overlap with other work.

The official schedule and project page disagree on the proposal-revision date, giving April 8 and April 6 respectively. An independent route need not pretend to follow a 2022 calendar. Preserve the order with four gates instead: problem and success metric; runnable baseline; stress test and failure analysis; frozen result and report. Every gate must point to a repository commit, figure, or short video. Partial results and unfinished directions may be reported; hiding failures and showing only the best rollout is the real defect.

Narrow the title to one central claim, such as “this swing-up controller is more robust than a baseline over a specified disturbance set,” rather than “build a quadrotor control system.” State the minimum model, control inputs, evaluation metric, and one experiment capable of falsifying the claim before adding perception, hardware, or environment complexity. The final package should rebuild at least one key figure on a machine with no access to MIT-private services.

## The boundary with 6.4210: dynamic control or robotic manipulation

[MIT 6.4210 Robotic Manipulation](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-4210-robotic-manipulation-fall-2022/) integrates perception, planning, and control around manipulation tasks; it better fits learners interested in grasping, geometry, object interaction, and a complete robotics pipeline. 6.832 instead makes underactuation, nonlinear dynamics, stability, trajectory optimization, contact, and uncertainty its spine. It often simplifies perception in order to deepen the closed-loop argument.

Choose 6.4210 first when the goal is to assemble a robot software stack that completes a manipulation task. Choose 6.832 first when the goal is to explain why a policy can drive or stabilize a dynamical system. They can be taken in sequence, but a 6.4210 perception/planning project should not masquerade as 6.832 stability training, and a simulated 6.832 trajectory is not a complete manipulation system.

## An auditable definition of completion

A credible independent record contains three prerequisite diagnostics, seven first attempts, seven correction memos, a locked execution environment, tests and logs for every notebook, one assumption ledger spanning the course, and a project package that passes all four gates. The project package includes the proposal, baseline, failure cases, final video, report, contribution statement, and reproduction command.

The boundary statement is also a deliverable. List the unavailable Gradescope, Piazza, staff review, and official grades; identify code taken from the public repository, compatibility migrations, and EEDIY-authored tests. Those records demonstrate an independent reproduction of public course content and the ability to support control claims. They do not represent MIT enrollment, credit, or certification.
