## How 18 Lectures Join Compression to Communication

The [official outline](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee276/outline.html) estimates 18 lectures. The first portion builds entropy, conditional and joint entropy, relative entropy, mutual information, AEP, and typicality. Prefix codes, Shannon codes, the Kraft inequality, Huffman coding, entropy rates, and universal compression then answer how many bits a source needs. Channel capacity, continuous information, AWGN, and the direct and converse sides of channel coding ask how much can be communicated reliably through noise. Polar codes, rate distortion, the method of types, and strong/joint typicality close the sequence.

Do not flatten this into a glossary. Maintain a theorem-dependency sheet that records random variables, distributions, block length, mode of convergence, and the inequality behind every result. AEP first turns long sequences into a typical-set counting problem; the source-coding converse then prices an attempt to code below entropy. The direct side of capacity constructs a feasible code, while the converse pushes every code back under a mutual-information bound. Notes containing conclusions but no quantifiers or failure conditions will not support rate distortion or the method of types.

### Pass a Proof Gate Before Opening a Solution

1. Given a small joint pmf, independently compute H(X), H(X|Y), and I(X;Y), then use KL divergence or the chain rule to explain why I(X;Y) is nonnegative.
2. For a convexity problem, select Jensen, log-sum, or the data-processing inequality correctly and state the equality condition instead of giving only a numerical example.
3. Explain what the law of large numbers controls in AEP, distinguish convergence in probability from pointwise equality, and carry out one Gaussian-density and differential-entropy manipulation.

If task 1 mishandles zero-probability support, review discrete probability, conditional distributions, and expectation. If task 2 produces inequality names without an argument, review convexity and elementary proof. If task 3 treats finite-block frequency as an exact limiting probability, review convergence of random variables. For each new topic, reason backward from the theorem to its assumptions and find a counterexample after deleting one. Preserve proof skeletons, codebooks or algorithms for constructions, and exact expressions, approximations, and error sources for calculations.

## Turn 8 Solutions into Delayed Feedback

The [official homework page](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee276/homework.html) lists HW1–HW8 with a question PDF and solution PDF for every set. HW3 reaches arithmetic coding, HW5 uses Fano's inequality, and HW6 includes polar decoding. Do not follow the webpage by opening both files together. Download questions, move solutions out of the working directory, complete an unaided attempt, and label each problem as definition, proof, construction, computation, or counterexample. After 24 hours, compare with the solution, record the first false premise, close the answer, and solve the entire problem again.

## Two Exams Matter Only Under Closed Conditions

The [official exams page](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee276/exam.html) publishes problem-and-solution PDFs for the midterm and final. Take them blind under 2-hour and 3-hour limits first. On a second pass, use solutions only to construct a rubric separating omitted assumptions, wrong direction, proof gaps, arithmetic errors, and insufficient exposition. After 7 days, redo missed problems without the answer. A public solution reveals the intended argument, but it cannot reproduce Gradescope partial credit, TA questions about an ambiguous proof, or the room conditions.

Correction is not transcription. Split the original into givens, target, key inequality, equality condition, and conclusion; mark the first failure. A wrong model is not rescued by matching algebra. Retake from cause labels alone.

Let the two blind tests divide the course. Use HW1–HW3 for measures, AEP, and source coding, and take the midterm at the capacity/AWGN boundary. Then continue through HW4–HW8, polar coding, rate distortion, and types, reserving the final as a closed cumulative check. If a re-solve can repeat official prose but cannot change a distribution or parameter, the feedback loop remains open.

## Leave the Finite-Block Experiment Until the End

The EEDIY supplement is “finite-block coding against the BSC capacity bound,” not an official Stanford project. Derive capacity `1-h2(p)`, then implement repetition, a random linear code, and a small polar code. Under the same channel crossover probability, sweep block length, rate, and decoding error while preserving code construction, seeds, raw trial counts, and confidence intervals. Acceptance requires an independent encoder/decoder round trip, boundary tests at `p=0` and near `p=0.5`, an explanation of empirical error versus the theoretical bound, and one-command plot reproduction. Use synthetic bits and simulated channels only, with no real personal data or communications.
