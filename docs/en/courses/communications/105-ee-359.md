### Course Fit and Prerequisites

Stanford [EE 359 Winter 2020](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee359/), taught by
Andrea Goldsmith, requires EE 279 or equivalent preparation according to the
[course info](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee359/courseinfo.html). It moves
from propagation/fading, capacity, diversity, and adaptive modulation into
MIMO, OFDM, spread spectrum, and multiuser systems. It fits learners with
probability, random processes, digital communications, and SVD who want design
tradeoffs grounded in channel assumptions. Use a basic communications course
first for initial AWGN, complex baseband, or symbol-error study.

### Assignments and feedback

The [lectures](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee359/lectures.html) provide 20
slide sets and summaries; videos enter Canvas and are not anonymously public.
For each lecture, build a model card with channel distribution, slow/fast,
flat/frequency-selective behavior, CSI, power/bandwidth constraint, and
metric. The 2020 LTE/5G examples explain theory but are not current standards.

The [homework page](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee359/homework.html)
publishes Homework 1–8 with solutions, and coding questions require scripts.
Freeze the first attempt, then preserve random seed, parameter sweep, units,
and earliest error during correction. Public solutions omit Canvas grades,
Piazza corrections, and staff feedback.

### Finish with a Narrow Project Question

The [projects](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee359/projects.html) list 2020
topics and earlier work. An outside project should ask a falsifiable question,
such as how diversity combining changes outage under specified fading and CSI,
not “implement 5G.” Derive a SISO Rayleigh or MRC baseline, then report Monte
Carlo sample count, confidence interval, and failure condition. MIMO/OFDM work
also records matrix dimensions, power allocation, cyclic prefix, and
synchronization. Separate Goldsmith theory, 2020 course material, and personal
implementation, preserving a case where analysis and simulation disagree.
Keep the theoretical assumptions, simulation parameters, and plotted axes
aligned, with both a nominal point and one boundary case. If a changed random
seed, normalization, or channel definition overturns the claim, keep that
failure and narrow the claim instead of adding more wireless terminology.

EE 359 ultimately teaches model judgment: identify the fading, CSI, and power
normalization behind an outage or BER curve, then predict where the conclusion
breaks when one condition changes.
