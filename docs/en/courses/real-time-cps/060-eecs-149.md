## Two Timelines Must Not Become One “Open Course”

The durable core of Berkeley EECS 149 is Edward A. Lee and Sanjit A. Seshia's *Introduction to Embedded Systems: A Cyber-Physical Systems Approach*. The [authors' book page](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/books/leeseshia/index.html) currently offers a free download of Second Edition version 2.3 (2017). It is marked all rights reserved, so link to the original PDF rather than copying the book into EEDIY. The text has 3 parts that can be studied concurrently and 7 two-chapter segments for a 14-week semester. Its subject is CPS modeling, design, and analysis, not a particular development board.

Keep that maintained text separate from the Fall 2015 EECS 149 archive used for pacing. The archived course was 4 units, with 3 lecture hours and 3 lab hours each week. Official prerequisites were EECS 16B or 20, 61C, and EECS 70 or Math 55. The archive links Spring 2012 and Spring 2011 screen captures and says the Fall 2016 class moved to bCourses. Those older captures are not recordings of the 2015 offering; that migration says nothing about current recording access. A downloadable textbook therefore does not imply public access to the matching course, video, hardware labs, or answers.

## How the Book's Semantics Map onto the 2015 Schedule

The [official logistics page](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/chess/eecs149/logistics.html) gives a tentative grading split: final project 30%, labs 9%, midterm 1 at 20%, midterm 2 at 20%, the best 6 of 7 homework sets at 18%, and participation 3%. bCourses, Piazza, physical lab checkoff, and team review were institutional feedback. An independent learner can use the proportions to allocate effort, but they are not a public credentialing rule, and no one outside the course should claim participation or staff acceptance.

The [official lecture schedule](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/chess/eecs149/lectures/index.html) starts with sensors, actuators, and continuous dynamics; moves through memory/I/O, discrete, modal, and hybrid systems; and then covers extended/timed automata, composition, hierarchical state machines, temporal logic, equivalence and refinement, reachability, and model checking. Its second half treats multitasking, OS and microkernels, scheduling anomalies, execution time, synchronous/reactive/dataflow models, security, and networks. Two midterms occurred in class, but the public schedule does not expose same-version papers or solutions. The spine is a provable relationship across semantic layers, not three disconnected surveys of embedded C, control, and formal methods.

### The Ledger of Old Homework and Old Hardware

The [official homework index](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/chess/eecs149/homework/index.html) lists 7 sets based on textbook chapters, with additional exercises for graduate students and no public answer key. Useful independent feedback comes from writing assumptions and a derivation before searching, re-deriving the result the next day, and checking it with simulation, units, limiting cases, or a model checker. When two attempts differ, preserve the discrepancy as a review item instead of silently overwriting the first record.

The [official labs and project page](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/chess/eecs149/project.html) describes a specific 2015 environment. Lab 1 used a Bluetooth WiiMote and accelerometer calibration. Lab 2 connected NI myRIO with Eclipse, Xilinx SDK, and LabVIEW/MicroBlaze. Lab 3 used bare-metal C, interrupts, tone generation, and an ADC. Lab 4–5 used a C state machine, CyberSim, and Kobuki simulation/deployment for hill climbing. Lab 6 moved to Statecharts/LabVIEW. Lab 7 addressed project management, followed by a 3–4 person final team project with live demo, presentation, report, and short video. The internal lab rubric was prelab 10, checkout 45, and report 45. Missing the legacy hardware, proprietary software, or staff checkout means a port is not completion of the original lab.

## Preserve the Model and Replace the Device During Migration

1. Derive a discrete update from a first-order plant, name sample time and task period separately, and explain which approximation fails first when they differ.
2. Draw a two-state controller as a state machine, state one invariant and one temporal property, and give an input sequence that violates one of them.
3. Explain a race between an interrupt and a main loop sharing state, then run one rate-monotonic schedulability check on periodic tasks.

If task 1 fails, review differential equations, sampling, and signals and systems. If task 2 yields a flowchart but no property, review automata and logic. If task 3 ends at “add a mutex” without a deadline calculation, review concurrency and real-time scheduling. Afterward, read version 2.3 in the sequence “continuous dynamics and sensing; discrete/modal/hybrid models; composition and formal properties; execution time and scheduling; concurrency, networks, and security.” For every block, retain one model, derivation, executable counterexample, and correction record. Let closed evidence loops determine pace instead of forcing the 2015 weekly calendar.

### A Robot Path and a No-Robot Path Produce Different Evidence

The [Lingua Franca embedded labs](https://www.lf-lang.org/embedded-lab/) descend from multiple generations of EECS 149 teaching. They use C, Lingua Franca coordination, and RP2040 on a Pololu 3pi+ 2040 robot for reactive and real-time control. This is a modern substitute, not the official Fall 2015 graded labs, and full exercises still require the specified robot. Without hardware, preserve the scheduler, sensor, and actuator interfaces in deterministic simulation and state that Bluetooth, ADC, motor-driver, mechanical-friction, and battery behavior were not validated.

Physical reconstruction of old Lab 1–7 belongs only in a supervised, low-voltage, current-limited space with an emergency stop. Do not operate a Kobuki or 3pi+ in public traffic, and do not connect a controller to a vehicle, medical device, power grid, or any system with material safety consequences. Publicly defensible completion evidence includes annotated book chapters, blind attempts and checks for 7 homework sets, state-machine and timing models, scheduling tests, simulation traces, and fault-injection logs. Under the official archive inventory, it does not include a same-offering 2015 recording; it also excludes bCourses/Piazza, official answers, solutions to the two 2015 midterms, proprietary toolchains, physical checkout, and team grading.

## Choose CU Boulder When the Goal Is POSIX Real-Time Service

The [CU Boulder Coursera course](https://www.coursera.org/learn/real-time-embedded-systems-concepts-practices) focuses more tightly on rate-monotonic scheduling, POSIX threads, Embedded Linux, and Raspberry Pi 3B+, arranged as 4 modules and 11 assignments. Full access to video, assignments, or a certificate may depend on a paid certificate, free trial, or the platform's current full-course option. Prefer Berkeley for hybrid models, temporal logic, refinement, and model checking. Choose CU Boulder for hands-on POSIX/RT services and Linux timing when a supported Raspberry Pi is available. The latter does not replace the former's CPS semantics.

### EEDIY Adds Only a Simulation Capstone

The EEDIY supplement is a “discrete-event scheduler plus first-order hybrid plant,” explicitly not an official Berkeley final project. In simulation only, implement rate-monotonic and EDF policies and inject jitter, missed deadlines, sensor dropout, and mode changes. Compare analytic response-time predictions against deadline misses and a safety invariant reconstructed from the event log. Deliver model assumptions, scheduler and plant sources, pinned seeds, raw events, property checks, analytic/simulation discrepancies, and failed cases. Completion requires a third party to reproduce every scenario with one command, trigger a check by deliberately breaking priorities, and pass regression after the fix. That is stronger CPS evidence than a robot that merely appears to move.
