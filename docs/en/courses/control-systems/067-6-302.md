## Treat the course as a closed-loop design review

The best entry to 6.302 is not a definition of feedback but a design that can fail. Given a motor or op-amp plant \(P(s)\), sensor \(H(s)\), actuator saturation, and target bandwidth, draw the complete loop first: identify where reference, disturbance, measurement noise, control effort, and output enter. Then write \(S=(1+L)^{-1}\), \(T=L(1+L)^{-1}\), and \(L=PCH\), explaining what higher low-frequency loop gain improves and which noise, saturation, or robustness problem it moves elsewhere. If you can manipulate the closed-loop formula but cannot identify where these signals enter the loop, return to block diagrams, LTI systems, and Laplace transforms.

MIT's [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-302-feedback-systems-spring-2007/pages/syllabus/) identifies Spring 2007 6.302 as a graduate introduction to feedback-system design. The formal background is listed as one of 6.003, 2.003, or 16.04. It also assumes circuit-laboratory experience such as 6.002, 6.071, or 16.04, retained physics from 8.01/8.02, and some exposure to complex variables. The loop review above is an EEDIY diagnostic, not an official MIT prerequisite exam.

## Every design must pass 5 gates

Gate 1 is a model audit: derive the plant from circuit laws or torque balance and list neglected poles, delay, friction, saturation, and sensor dynamics; do not infer a convenient model backward from a desired Bode plot. Gate 2 is a stability audit: use root locus and Nyquist under the same sign convention, retaining the encirclement count, number of right-half-plane poles, and resulting closed-loop poles.

Gate 3 is a loop-shaping audit: assign frequency regions for tracking, disturbance rejection, noise, control effort, and robustness before adding lead/lag or a minor loop; gain and phase margin are not isolated scores. Gate 4 is a nonlinearity audit: inspect actuator limits, integrator windup, dead zone, rate limits, and dynamics missed by a describing function. Gate 5 is an implementation audit: make a worst-case argument for component tolerance, sampling or computation delay, op-amp GBW, slew rate, supply range, and grounding.

These 5 gates match the course's distinctive center. 6.302 asks for more than closed-loop poles in the left half-plane: the designer must explain a physical plant, frequency tradeoffs, and an actual amplifier. Attach every problem solution or lab memo to at least 1 gate. A MATLAB plot without a plant equation, specification, and failure explanation does not complete the review. A model record should also identify the linearization point and amplitude range: the same motor near friction deadband or the same op-amp near output swing may violate the paper model. Label whether each frequency plot is open or closed loop, input and output units, phase unwrapping, and neglected dynamics; after changing a compensator, recheck every channel instead of displaying only the improved curve.

## 6.302 and CDS/FBS divide the work; one does not replace the other

The [Feedback Systems 2e page](https://fbswiki.org/wiki/index.php/FBS) by Åström and Murray publishes the complete second edition dated 2020-07-24 and reports a 2024 update of Python figure sources for python-control 0.10.1. Its 15 chapters run from modeling, state/output feedback, observability, and Kalman filtering through loop shaping, robust performance, fundamental limits, and architectures. That breadth is closer to the Caltech CDS 101/110 and modern-control path. The FBS page is a book and supplement entry, not an official replacement semester of 6.302.

What 6.302 uniquely supplies is the electronic and electromechanical density of classical feedback: Hall/Nichols, the Bode obstacle course, internal and minor-loop op-amp compensation, PLLs, and servomotor labs all point toward return ratio and compensation in a physical loop. For a first transfer-function-to-hardware loop review, start with 6.302. If Nyquist and lead/lag are already fluent and the target is state estimation, state feedback, robustness, and architecture, move to FBS/CDS. A modern control project can use FBS to close 6.302's state-space gap, but it should retain the actuator, sensor, and saturation audit.

## Four diagnostics decide where remediation belongs

Diagnostic 1: derive a second-order plant from motor electrical and mechanical parameters, explaining whether sensor and amplifier poles belong in \(L(s)\). Diagnostic 2: for a loop with a right-half-plane pole, complete a Nyquist encirclement count under a stated contour and check it against root locus. Diagnostic 3: design a lead compensator for a specified crossover, calculating phase margin, low-frequency error, and high-frequency noise amplification. Diagnostic 4: given saturation and delay, explain why linear margins are insufficient and propose testable limits.

A failure on item 1 calls for circuits, mechanics, and modeling; item 2 calls for complex analysis and Bode/Nyquist sign conventions; item 3 calls for frequency response and sensitivity functions; item 4 calls for nonlinear and implementation limits. Complete at least 3 with a derivation, cross-check, and unit audit without an answer reference before beginning a Lab 2 design. These thresholds are an EEDIY placement rule.
