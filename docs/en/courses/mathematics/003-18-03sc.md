## Treat 18.03SC as Input–State–Response, Not a Catalog of Methods

The MIT [course home](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/)
identifies the Fall 2011 OCW Scholar edition presented by Arthur Mattuck,
Haynes Miller, Jeremy Orloff, and John Lewis. The public edition has 38
sessions, each combining written notes, lecture video, problem-solving video,
practice problems, and problem sets. MIT estimates more than 3 hours on
average per session and about 150 hours total. That describes full
participation, not a requirement that every learner watch at one speed.

The course is easily misread as a template list for separable, first-order
linear, second-order constant-coefficient, and Laplace problems. A more useful
spine is to derive an equation from physical quantities and conservation,
separate input from state, solve transient and steady response, and check
units, limits, plots, and initial values. In EE that spine leads directly to
RC/LRC models, sinusoidal gain/phase, poles, convolution, and state-space.
The course does not include discrete time, sampling, the Z-transform, or
communication systems.

## Use One RC, One Complex Exponential, and One 2×2 System at Entry

The official [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/syllabus/)
requires 18.01, makes 18.02 a corequisite, and expects vectors, matrix
arithmetic, and vector-valued functions. Perform three EEDIY diagnostics;
they are not an official MIT entrance test:

1. From \(C\,dv/dt=(V_s-v)/R\), identify state, input, time constant, and
   units; solve the step response without a formula sheet; check \(t=0^+\)
   and \(t\to\infty\). If you can write the final expression but cannot justify
   it, review derivatives, exponentials, and Kirchhoff modeling first.
2. Compute the magnitude and phase of \(e^{j\omega t}\) through a first-order
   linear equation and recover the real signal. If polar form, Euler's
   identity, or phase convention is unstable, review complex-number basics.
3. For a \(2\times2\) matrix with two distinct real eigenvalues, find
   eigenpairs, draw nullclines and the phase portrait, and explain how the
   initial value selects modal coefficients. If arithmetic and geometry are
   disconnected, review linear algebra.

Item 1 diagnoses modeling, item 2 frequency response, and item 3 coupled
systems. Put the results in the learning log instead of reducing readiness to
the name of a prior calculus course.

## Model First, Then Let Four Units Add Representations

The official [Unit I](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/unit-i-first-order-differential-equations/)
moves from first-order ODEs, direction fields, Euler's method, linear
equations, and autonomous equations into modeling. Unit II covers
second-order constant-coefficient linear equations, oscillation, forcing,
resonance, complex exponentials, and frequency response. Unit III joins
periodic inputs, Fourier series, Laplace transforms, and convolution to the
same response question. Unit IV rewrites higher-order equations as
first-order systems and studies eigenvalues, phase portraits, matrix
exponentials, and nonlinear linearization.

Do not reorder that course-specific chain. Unit I establishes “quantity to
equation” and reality checks. Unit II separates transient/steady and
amplitude/phase. Unit III compares Fourier and Laplace decompositions of an
input. Unit IV finally puts scalar transfer and state representations side by
side. Starting from a Laplace table leaves invisible gaps in initial values,
causality, and physical units.

For each model preserve a four-line audit: which relation produced the
equation, when the initial condition applies, what homogeneous and particular
terms mean, and how units, limits, or numerical integration check the result.
The hardest and most useful task is not root finding; it is deciding from
plots, poles, and phase portraits whether growth, decay, oscillation,
resonance, and stability match the original system.

## Part I and Part II Are Not Substitutes

The public Scholar problem sets divide each collection into Part I for basic
skills and Part II for longer multistep and realistic modeling problems.
Complete solutions exist for both. Attempt Part I without consulting
solutions; if one skill fails
twice, return to its session. Move to Part II only after Part I is stable, and
require a reality check on every model. Part I alone reduces the course to
symbol manipulation; Part II alone lets algebra obscure model judgment.

Units I–III each have a practice exam, practice solution, exam, and solution.
By the official
[Exam 3](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/unit-iii-fourier-series-and-laplace-transform/exam-3/),
you should be able to choose among Fourier series, Laplace transforms, and
convolution under time pressure, not only after a keyword prompts a table
lookup. Keep the timed first attempt and classify
losses as modeling, method selection, algebra, initial value, unit, or
interpretation. After reading a solution, redo a variant with changed parameters.
The public pages do not list an off-campus automatic grader; PDF solutions
provide feedback, not an MIT grade.

Mathlets are official interactive Java demonstrations from Fall 2011; treat
them as legacy tools. If a current browser cannot run them, substitute a
local static or numerical direction-field/phase-portrait plot rather than
weakening browser security. Preserve equation, parameters, integration step,
and software version; a screenshot alone is not reproducible.

## Unit IV Exits Through the Cumulative Final, Not Another Unit Exam

The official [Unit IV](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/unit-iv-first-order-systems/)
places systems last. In the public structure, Units I–III each have 1 unit
exam, and Unit IV leads directly to the
[Final Exam](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/final-exam/).
The final page supplies a practice final and solution plus the final and
solution. The completion gate is therefore 3 unit exams and 1 cumulative
final, not an invented fourth unit exam.

At the end of Unit IV, express one second-order LRC model in four
cross-checked forms: time-domain ODE, complex-frequency transfer expression,
convolution/impulse response, and a 2-state first-order system. This
four-representation dossier is **an EEDIY supplement, not an official MIT
assignment**. Use only a public model or synthetic parameters and connect no
physical circuit. Submit derivations, poles and units, numerical step and
sinusoidal responses, error plots, a locked environment, and one rerun
command.

## Choose Between 18.03SC and 6.003 by Their Scope

The Fall 2011 [6.003 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/)
treats both continuous-time and discrete-time signals and adds the
Z-transform, sampling, modulation, LTI representations, feedback,
communication, and signal processing. 18.03SC asks how to construct, solve,
and interpret differential equations. 6.003 asks how unified signal and
system representations analyze transforms, sampling, and interconnection.
Choose 18.03SC first when ODEs, complex exponentials, and convolution are not
stable. Move to 6.003 once you can explain how poles determine an impulse
response and want to study discrete time, frequency-domain reasoning, and
system design. The overlap is a bridge, not duplication.
