### Course fit

MIT [18.03SC](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/) is not a catalogue of equation tricks. It organizes first-order equations, second-order oscillation, Fourier and Laplace methods, and linear systems as different views of input, state, and response—the language later used in circuits, control, and signals. The official [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/syllabus/) requires single-variable calculus and recommends basic matrix fluency. If separation of variables is possible but initial conditions, units, and long-term behavior remain hard to explain, start at the beginning rather than jumping to a Laplace table.

### Course structure

Unit I uses direction fields, numerical methods, and first-order models to ask how state changes. Unit II develops second-order systems, resonance, and complex notation. Unit III moves to the frequency domain with Fourier series and Laplace transforms. Unit IV rewrites higher-order problems as first-order linear systems. Keep the same physical object—such as a driven RLC or mass–spring–damper system—through the sequence and translate it among a differential equation, phase portrait, frequency response, and state-space model. The comparison reveals what each representation exposes and whether a parameter or initial condition disappeared during a conversion.

Leave four lines beside every solution: the state variables and their units, initial or boundary conditions, the input's time support, and behavior as \(t\to0\) and \(t\to\infty\). The homogeneous solution, particular solution, and numerical trajectory must use the same parameters. Calculate zero-input and zero-state responses separately and confirm that they reconstruct the total response. At resonance, distinguish growth in an ideal undamped model from the finite peak of a damped system. In a Laplace problem, place poles, initial-condition terms, and causality on a single sketch so a formally correct table lookup cannot conceal a physically impossible result.

Course topics usually place explanation, examples, and problems close together. Do not treat Part I and Part II as playback milestones. Write the model and expected limiting behavior, attempt the problem, then use the solution to decide whether the failure was modeling, method choice, or algebra. [Exam 3](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/unit-iii-fourier-series-and-laplace-transform/exam-3/) is a useful check on whether Fourier and Laplace reasoning has gone beyond table lookup.

### Take a Second-Order System through Every Representation

Choose a second-order system with explicit parameters. Derive its free and forced responses, draw its phase portrait and magnitude response, then rewrite it as a first-order state equation. Change a set of damping or drive-frequency values and explain how the poles, transient, and steady state move together. Include an analytically checkable special case and a step-size convergence result. Numerical integration may verify the result but should not hide the analytic structure.

Finish with the public [final exam](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/final-exam/) under a time limit. If 6.003 is next, compare its [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/): 18.03SC establishes mathematical representations of dynamic systems, while 6.003 systematizes LTI analysis, convolution, and frequency-domain reasoning. The real dividing line is not whether you can look up a transform, but whether you recognize the same system in a differential equation, phase portrait, frequency response, and state-space model.
