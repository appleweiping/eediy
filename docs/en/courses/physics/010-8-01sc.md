### Course approach

Choose MIT [8.01SC](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/) for a first systematic mechanics course, particularly before motors, robotics, or electromechanical systems. This is the Fall 2016 Scholar edition, whose 37 numbered lessons move from kinematics, Newton's laws, and constraints through momentum, work and energy, collisions, rigid-body rotation, angular momentum, and rolling. Two calculations run through the material: the force account starts with a system boundary, free-body diagram, and \(\sum\mathbf F=d\mathbf p/dt\); the energy account checks the result using work, kinetic and potential energy, and dissipation. When they disagree, the missing item is usually an external force, frictional work, collision assumption, or rolling constraint—not another formula.

### Prerequisites

The official [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/syllabus/) lists no formal prerequisite and makes 18.01SC a corequisite. Before starting, be able to explain velocity and acceleration from an \(x(t)\) graph, draw a free-body diagram containing only real interactions, and state the system before deciding whether momentum or kinetic energy is conserved in a collision.

Draw the boundary and positive coordinates before choosing dynamics, momentum, or energy. “Centripetal force” is not an extra force to add. Mechanical-energy conservation requires an account of nonconservative work. A rotation problem requires an axis and moment of inertia before algebra. These judgments transfer to motor shafts, flywheels, actuators, and IMU reference frames, but 8.01SC does not teach electromagnetic torque, sensor electronics, or feedback control.

Three models are worth carrying through the course. A block and incline separates internal forces, external forces, and constraints. A two-body collision separates momentum conservation, energy loss, and center-of-mass motion. A rolling body puts translation, rotation, static friction, and geometric constraint in the same problem. Draw a free-body diagram, keep both momentum and energy accounts, and test a limiting case for each: vanishing friction, an extreme mass ratio, or a changed rotational inertia. In the rotation block, check angular momentum about different reference points and explain when the expression changes without changing the physical prediction. This reveals the system boundaries beneath familiar formulas.

### Assignments and feedback

The official [Assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/assignments/) page lists Problem Sets 1–12 and related worked examples for most weeks. Those examples demonstrate nearby modeling decisions; they are not solutions to every assigned problem. Complete an independent derivation first, then use an example to check the boundary, conservation conditions, signs, and units. Leave an unresolved question visible rather than importing an unattributed answer key.

The [Online Textbook](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/online-textbook/) has 31 chapters and extends beyond the 12-week spine into oscillation, fluids, and non-inertial frames. Use it for a second explanation, not as another mandatory checklist. If this presentation is too slow, compare the faster, more derivational [8.012 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-012-physics-i-classical-mechanics-fall-2008/pages/syllabus/).

A useful synthesis uses a low-risk motion clip or synthetic trajectory. Explain it once with forces and once with energy or momentum, compare the model with \(x(t)\) or \(\theta(t)\), and show how numerical step size creates conservation drift. The analysis should answer three concrete questions: where is the system boundary, how does the environment act, and which conserved quantity can independently check the answer?
