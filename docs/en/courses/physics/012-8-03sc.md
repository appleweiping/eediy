## Start with a phase check before Lecture 1

Before playing Lecture 1, write the steady-state complex amplitude of a driven damped oscillator and answer 3 questions: how does phase change as the drive crosses resonance, how does quality factor \(Q\) relate to half-power bandwidth, and which quantities are merely renamed—and which physical boundaries truly change—when the mass–spring–damper becomes a series RLC circuit? If you can recall only \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\) but cannot work with complex impedance or distinguish the transient from the steady-state response, review second-order differential equations, phasors, and basic circuits first.

## The 24 lectures are not 24 unrelated topics

The official sequence contains 24 complete lecture videos with aligned notes. Lectures 1–11 move from harmonic, damped, and driven motion through coupled oscillators, translation symmetry, the wave equation, traveling waves, and sound. Lectures 12–16 carry the same structure into Maxwell's equations, electromagnetic waves, dispersion, group velocity, Fourier transforms, and propagation in 2 or 3 dimensions. ## Read the 3 parts through 4 continuity checks

Place the first gate after Lecture 3: derive magnitude and phase response from the differential equation, separately labeling the underdamped transient, steady-state gain, resonance frequency, and energy dissipation. Place the second after Lecture 8: find the eigenfrequencies and eigenvectors of 2 coupled oscillators, then explain why an infinite periodic chain produces a dispersion relation instead of merely drawing “two peaks.”

The third gate follows Lecture 13: derive a reflection/transmission or standing-wave relation from boundary conditions and write both phase and group velocity; treating them as one number means returning to dispersive media. The fourth follows Lecture 22: use one phase ledger to explain polarization, thin-film interference, array factor, and single-slit diffraction, specifying when field amplitudes add and when intensities add. Retain the derivation, dimensional check, and one physical interpretation plot at every gate.

This order makes each new object inherit an earlier model: one oscillator supplies pole intuition, finite coupling supplies modes, the continuum limit supplies wavenumber, boundaries supply reflection, and multiple paths supply interference. Notes that only copy definitions lecture by lecture miss these 5 transfers and discard the course's central structure.

## Its interfaces to the EE spine are specific

Oscillator poles, damping ratio, and \(Q\) recur in RLC circuits, resonators, filters, and closed loops. Modes and dispersion in a periodic chain connect to transmission lines, lattices, and waveguides. Fourier decomposition, phase/group velocity, and sampling intuition lead into signals and DSP. Polarization, Fresnel relations, interference, and diffraction lead into fields, antenna arrays, and photonics. For each transfer, make a “state variable–parameter–conservation/dissipation–boundary condition” crosswalk instead of relying on an analogy word.

In the other direction, 8.03SC does not replace signals and systems: it does not organize LTI properties, convolution, causality, ROC, and discrete-time realization as a course. It does not replace electromagnetics because transmission lines, waveguide modes, constitutive models, and antenna boundary problems are not developed as engineering design topics. It also does not replace a laboratory; watching demonstrations does not establish instrument use, calibration, or uncertainty analysis. Its natural position is after 8.02 and differential equations and before signals or electromagnetics.

The course does not require code. EEDIY separately recommends 1 unofficial computational notebook: plot the driven oscillator's magnitude and phase and estimate \(Q\) from half-power points; extend 2 oscillators to \(N\) periodically coupled cells and compare discrete eigenfrequencies with a continuum dispersion curve; then observe standing waves under finite-length boundaries. Every plot should retain parameters, units, an analytic prediction, numerical error, and at least 1 failed case.

Current Python with NumPy/SciPy, Julia, or MATLAB/Octave is acceptable; none is an MIT course requirement. Do not hide numerical stability to obtain a smooth animation, and do not estimate “group velocity” by eye from a moving peak.
