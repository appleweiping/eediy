## The native spine is one path from a thermistor divider to submitted data

Module 1 introduces thermal sensors including thermistors, RTDs, and thermocouples. Module 2 moves into PSoC prototyping, pins, amplifiers, MUX, DAC, ADC, C code, and a 4-part thermistor lab. Module 3 extends the view to rotary encoders, resolvers, and flow sensors. Module 4 treats inverting, non-inverting, summing, difference, and instrumentation amplifiers, nonidealities, and Johnson, 1/f, shot, and quantization noise. Module 5 closes with thermistor data collection, screenshots, and the course-project assessment. This is the official page's 5-part structure; it should not be rewritten as a generic sensor encyclopedia.

Keep evidence along one measurement chain: temperature → thermistor resistance → divider voltage → analog front end → ADC code → C conversion → LCD/readout → calibrated data. At each transition, record the transfer function, range, loading, noise sources, quantization, and calibration residual. Use Module 3 rotary and flow material to test whether the method transfers to another transducer. Do not invent an unrelated “smart sensor” and label it the official course project.

The main value is that Module 2 and Module 5 meet. Errors in pin configuration, ADC setup, C, and the thermistor reappear in the final data. That is also the likely sticking point: aligning schematic, firmware, reference resistor, ADC range, display, and raw data at one revision. A single successful screenshot cannot establish that conversion, calibration, and wiring were correct.

## Three desktop diagnostics determine whether theory must come first

Diagnostic 1: for a divider made of \(R_T\) and a known resistor, derive \(V_{ADC}(R_T)\), choose high- or low-side placement, derive the usable code range from the ADC reference, and explain self-heating as systematic error. Diagnostic 2: given instrumentation-amplifier gain, input common-mode range, offset, and noise density, determine whether a sensor signal saturates and estimate in-band noise. Diagnostic 3: write a small C flow that converts ADC code to temperature, handles out-of-range and error states, and formats a value for the LCD.

A failure on item 1 calls for basic circuits, loading, and calibration. A failure on item 2 calls for op-amp or instrumentation-amplifier behavior and noise integration. A failure on item 3 calls for embedded C, integer and floating-point conversion, and peripheral state. These are EEDIY placement checks, not Coursera quizzes. If 2 of the 3 cannot be completed independently, take the course ID 136 theory route before buying hardware.

## A lab record must trace raw data back to wiring

After obtaining the formal materials and hardware, do not wire the whole system in one pass. Verify schematic and pin map while unpowered; perform a board self-test under current-limited power; check the ADC transfer with a known voltage; retain raw ADC codes after adding the divider; only then fit the thermistor relation, display temperature, and run the Module 5 collection. At every step, retain the expected value, measurement, instrument setting, source revision, and anomaly handling.

Following the structure listed on the official course page, a completion package should contain access and status for all 5 modules; submission and feedback status for all 5 assignments; PSoC project sources; schematic; pin map; final BOM; raw thermistor data; calibration curve and residual; at least 1 oscilloscope capture with scale and probe information; LCD output; a version and bug log; and Module 5 course-project submission evidence. If Coursera disallows publication of prompts or submissions, record a hash, date, and non-answer reflection. Do not publish quiz answers or restricted prompts.

Calibration evidence cannot retain only converted temperature. Store raw ADC code, reference voltage, resistor tolerance, sample time, and environmental conditions together. After fitting, inspect whether residuals drift monotonically with temperature and distinguish model error, self-heating, contact resistance, power variation, and quantization. The LCD is an interface check, not measurement truth; a screenshot becomes meaningful only when every step from raw code to display is traceable.

If repeated measurements do not fall within the expected uncertainty, check contacts and supply first and only then reconsider the fit. Extra decimal places cannot compensate for poor repeatability.

The safety scope here is isolated, current-limited, ≤5 V low-energy bench circuitry, with power removed before rewiring. Do not connect a person, medical sensor, mains supply, unknown supply, exposed heater, or other high-temperature source. Prefer safe, stable, verifiable environmental changes as temperature inputs. A certificate or one correct reading is not product, medical, or safety certification.

## Combine it with ID 136 instead of pretending they are interchangeable

NPTEL [Electronic Measurement and Instrumentation](https://nptel.ac.in/courses/108105153) is the stronger open theory spine. Measurement error, instruments, bridges, transducers, and signal conditioning can be studied without this PSoC and BOM. ECEA 5340's advantage is the conditional practice chain joining a specific PSoC 5LP, thermistor lab, and course project. Its costs are uncertain paid or account access, particular hardware, and a legacy Windows toolchain. Choose ID 136 when theory must come first; after access and equipment are confirmed, use this course to close the firmware–ADC–calibration loop.
