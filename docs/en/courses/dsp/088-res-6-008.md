## Treat this as a digital replay of a 1987 course

The [RES.6-008 home](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-008-digital-signal-processing-spring-2011/) carries “As Taught In Spring 2011,” while its description also states that the MIT Center for Advanced Engineering Studies developed this distance-education course in 1987 for engineers and scientists in the workplace. Spring 2011 identifies the OCW resource; it does not mean that the blackboard lectures were recorded again or that the hardware context and textbook were updated in 2011.

The [Introduction](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-008-digital-signal-processing-spring-2011/pages/introduction/) requires advanced calculus, introductory complex variables, and previous continuous-time linear-systems work with Laplace and Fourier transforms. It assumes no prior discrete-time signals, z-transform, or DFT. Before starting, independently compute a continuous-time convolution, test whether a system is LTI, and explain frequency response with complex exponentials. If all 3 are missing, review signals and systems instead of expecting Lesson 1 to do both jobs.

## Replay it as representation → transform → realization → algorithm

Volume 1 is Lectures 1–7: difference equations, discrete convolution, DTFT, and z-transform. Build a 4-column ledger for every sequence—time-domain definition, DTFT, z-transform, and ROC. Causality and stability must be explained from the ROC, not left as one rational expression. Volume 2 is Lectures 8–10: DFS, DFT, and circular convolution. Use length-\(N\) periodic extension to explain why circular convolution is not “incorrect linear convolution,” then state when zero-padding makes the two agree.

Volume 3 is Lectures 11–17: linear digital networks, IIR/FIR structures, coefficient quantization, IIR design, Butterworth filters, and FIR design. When comparing direct, cascade, and parallel realizations, track pole/zero sensitivity, state scaling, overflow, and delay count rather than only confirming the same ideal \(H(z)\). Volume 4 is Lectures 18–20: DFT computation and FFT. Work through at least 1 decomposition by hand, retaining index permutation, twiddle factors, and operation count.

At the end of each block, show that several descriptions name the same object. A difference equation, impulse response, transfer function, and block diagram should all describe one system. Frequency-domain sampling, periodic extension, and circular convolution must use the same index convention. Filter specifications, pole-zero geometry, and the implementation structure should be mutually traceable. If the plots look similar but the delay, scaling, or ROC does not agree, stop and reconcile the representations before moving on.

The Introduction gives a provider estimate of about 5 hours per lesson and recommends video, then text, then problems. That number belongs to the historical distance course, not an EEDIY time promise. A safer progression rule is to freeze a first attempt at the current lesson's non-asterisked problems and check units, ROC, and index conventions before consulting the solution. The provider calls asterisked problems optional extensions.

## Four checks expose shaky understanding

Check 1: given a difference equation, initial-rest condition, and input, obtain the same output by recursion, convolution, and z-transform, stating the unilateral or bilateral convention. Check 2: given rational \(X(z)\), list possible ROCs and classify each as causal, right- or left-sided, and stable. Partial fractions alone do not complete it.

Check 3: compute both linear and \(N\)-point circular convolution for 2 finite sequences and give the minimum padding that avoids time aliasing. Check 4: implement one high-order IIR in direct form and second-order sections, comparing pole movement and output error after coefficient quantization and finite precision. The first 3 are core course ideas; the fourth connects Lecture 13 parameter quantization to a current realization. They are EEDIY placement diagnostics, not MIT exam questions.

Failure on check 1 calls for discrete-time LTI systems and transform conventions. Failure on check 2 means stopping at Lectures 5–7; check 3 means reworking Lectures 8–10; check 4 means returning to structures and numerical representation. Complete at least 3 independently with cross-checks before starting a self-designed project.

## RES.6-008 and 6.341 differ in depth and practice

MIT [6.341 Discrete-Time Signal Processing](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-341-discrete-time-signal-processing-fall-2005/) is a Fall 2005 graduate subject that explicitly presumes fluency with LTI systems, sampling, Fourier transforms, and bilateral z-transforms; its formal prerequisite is 6.011. Beyond the shared DFT, structures, and IIR/FIR base, it continues into decimation and interpolation, sample-rate conversion, linear prediction, short-time Fourier analysis, filter banks, multirate methods, Hilbert transforms, cepstral analysis, and applications.

Its public feedback is also different. 6.341 publishes Problem Sets 1–11 with solutions, exams with solutions, and 2 required MATLAB projects with written reports; the project files still refer to MIT Athena and an older MATLAB environment. RES.6-008's advantage is a 20-lesson path that introduces discrete time from the beginning and supplies solutions for the last 19 lessons. It publishes no exam, coding lab, or project. Choose RES.6-008 for a first DSP course; move to 6.341 after passing z/DFT/filter-design diagnostics when multirate, STFT, and project evidence are needed.

## The unofficial code lab only fills the runnable gap

EEDIY separately recommends 1 current-toolchain repository labeled as an unofficial supplement. Its minimum scope is: compute the DFT by definition and FFT and compare error and operation trend; test padding with overlap-safe convolution; design 1 IIR and 1 FIR from specifications; realize the high-order IIR in direct form and SOS; and sweep coefficient word length. Retain tests, frequency response, impulse response, pole-zero plots, and a failure note for each item.

Current Python with NumPy/SciPy, JuliaDSP, or MATLAB/Octave is acceptable; record versions and floating- or fixed-point assumptions. A library-generated plot is not a filter-design explanation. State passband and stopband, sampling frequency, normalization, phase, transient handling, and numerical tolerance. The course does not require real-time audio, a DSP board, or fixed-point deployment, so those extensions must not be described as MIT labs.
