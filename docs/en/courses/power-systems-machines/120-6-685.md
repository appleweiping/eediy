## Decide Whether the Missing Layer Is Power or Machine Theory

MIT 6.685 is James Kirtley’s Fall 2013 graduate Electric Machines course. It
uses machinery to teach electromechanics, including analysis and design of
major rotating and linear machines, estimation of dynamic parameters, and
system regulation, stability, controllability, and response. The official
[syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-685-electric-machines-fall-2013/pages/syllabus/)
requires 6.061/6.690 Introduction to Electric Power Systems or permission of
the instructor. That prerequisite is not an administrative detail.

The quickest placement exercise is one sheet that computes three-phase power
and a per-unit conversion from a phasor diagram, derives force or torque from
reluctance, flux, and coenergy, and uses an induction-machine equivalent
circuit to explain slip, power flow, and a torque-speed curve. Review
introductory power first if both three-phase analysis and magnetic circuits
are weak. A learner who knows
steady-state equivalents but has not used the Park transform can proceed, but
should budget a separate block for dynamic models. I would place 6.685 after
power-system analysis, not at the entrance to the power track.

## The Twelve Chapters Should Not Be Read at Constant Speed

The
[course-notes page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-685-electric-machines-fall-2013/pages/course-notes/)
provides 12 chapters as the primary reference, while warning that chapter
order does not exactly reproduce lecture order. The
[calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-685-electric-machines-fall-2013/pages/calendar/)
lists 24 lectures and restores the dependencies. I would divide the material
into five blocks:

- **Fields, energy, and materials:** Chapters 1–3 with Lectures 1–4 connect
  Maxwell stress, virtual work, magnetic circuits, eddy currents, and loss in
  one energy account.
- **The synchronous-machine language:** Lectures 3–8 interleave Chapters 4–5
  with round-rotor models, ratings, Park transforms, windings, and air-gap
  fields.
- **Induction, DC, and reluctance machines:** Chapter 8 accompanies Lectures
  9–11 on linear/induction models, transformer models, and torque-speed.
  Chapter 6 accompanies the DC-machine treatment in Lecture 12, while Lecture
  13 treats variable-reluctance principles.
- **Dynamics and drives:** Chapter 9 accompanies Lectures 14–16 on synchronous
  dynamics, per-unit quantities, and simulation. Chapter 10 goes with
  Lectures 17–18 on induction drives/control and doubly-fed machines; Chapter
  12 goes with Lecture 19 on single-phase induction motors.
- **Permanent magnets and the closing lectures:** Chapters 6–7 return in
  Lectures 20–21 for permanent-magnet systems and brushless DC. Chapter 11 is
  a design-synthesis reference, but the calendar does not assign it to one
  lecture. Lectures 22–24 revisit fields/power flow, special topics, and the
  wrap-up.

Perform a dimensional and limiting check in every block. What happens as an
air gap grows without bound, as slip approaches zero, or as the d- and q-axis
parameters become equal? Machine derivations can remain symbolically neat
while silently mixing phase and line quantities, peak and RMS values, or
mechanical and electrical angle. Put the convention at the top of every
derivation.

## How to Use the Eleven Sets and Two Quizzes

The
[assignments page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-685-electric-machines-fall-2013/pages/assignments/)
publishes Problem Sets 1–11 with a solution for every set. PS3 also includes
an excerpt of 2007 M-19 non-oriented silicon-steel data. Problems mix textbook
derivations with research-motivated calculations. The syllabus calls problem
sets the most important way to learn the material and recommends a program for
the heavier computation. Official solutions use MATLAB; Freemat, Maple, and
Mathcad were also named as possible tools in 2013. That does not guarantee
that old scripts execute unchanged today.

Work PS1–PS3 with force, magnetic circuits, and material/loss; PS4–PS7 with
synchronous machines, windings, induction, and DC machines; and PS8–PS11 with
reluctance machines, dynamic simulation, drives/control, and PM/BLDC. Redraw
the magnetic or equivalent circuit, state phase/line and peak/RMS conventions
and positive torque, then check power balance or a limiting operating
condition. After reading a solution, identify whether a discrepancy came from
topology, reference direction, parameter conversion, or numerical solution
instead of writing only “calculation error.”

The
[exams page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-685-electric-machines-fall-2013/pages/exams/)
publishes two 1.5-hour quizzes with solutions. The calendar says Quiz 1 covers
PS1–PS7 and Quiz 2 covers PS8–PS10. Although PS11 exists and the syllabus
mentions a three-hour final, OCW does not provide that final or its solution.
The two quizzes are therefore not a “complete exam archive,” and PS11 should
not be assigned an invented public exam. Without the three-hour final, the
public release cannot reproduce the original assessment in full.

## With No Official Project, Write a Two-Machine Calculation Report

This OCW release has no lab, project, or lecture video. Independent study can
still produce an integrated calculation report, but it must be labeled an EEDIY
supplement rather than an official MIT 6.685 final project. The scattered
calculations can become a paired synchronous- and induction-machine report.
Select parameters already given in the notes or problem sets, build
steady-state and dynamic models, and
report a torque-speed or power-angle curve, a start-up or load-step transient,
a loss/efficiency account, and parameter sensitivity.

State phase/line, peak/RMS, and mechanical/electrical-angle conventions before
writing the abc-to-dq transformation. A synchronous-machine curve should span
no-load, rated, and overload regions; an induction-machine curve should span
low-slip, nominal-slip, and high-slip regions rather than three arbitrary
points. For each machine and symbol convention, choose a matching solved
problem to check torque direction, power scale, and numerical values. Place an
energy or power residual beside the curves, and check whether the transient
changes with integration step or solver. Python/NumPy, GNU Octave, or current
MATLAB can carry the calculation. This added report is a public-study record,
not an official MIT project.

## The Public Calculation Route Does Not Require Energizing a Machine

The
[instructor insights](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-685-electric-machines-fall-2013/pages/instructor-insights/)
describe a Fall 2013 class of fewer than 10 students, a deliberately classical
approach, and research examples grounded in physical phenomena. They do not
publish a laboratory bench that an outside learner can reproduce. No motor,
inverter, encoder, or three-phase supply is required. Open numerical tools can
make software cost zero, while commercial MATLAB and reference books may cost
money.

Do not connect mains, a VFD, a three-phase motor, or a homemade high-speed
rotor merely because the equations look manageable. Shock, charged DC buses,
overcurrent, insulation failure, ejected parts, and stored mechanical energy
are not addressed by a notebook. Physical machine work belongs in a qualified
laboratory with rated protection, isolation/current limiting, emergency stop,
mechanical guarding, appropriate instruments, and expert supervision. This
guide provides no personal energization route. For public independent study,
the 11 sets, two quizzes, paired-machine report, and per-problem error record
can form a public-study record. Because the three-hour final is absent, that
record is not a complete reconstruction or proof of course completion.
Running a physical three-phase machine is not a public-material requirement
and cannot replace the missing exam.
