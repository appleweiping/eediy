### This Is a 2011 OCW Republication of a 1987 Video Course

MIT [RES.6-007 Signals and Systems](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-007-signals-and-systems-spring-2011/) is labeled Spring 2011, but the video course was produced for workplace distance education in 1987; its equipment and phrasing belong to that version. It suits learners willing to pair continuous- and discrete-time work and solve problems lecture by lecture. The official [introduction](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-007-signals-and-systems-spring-2011/pages/introduction/) assumes calculus, complex-number manipulation, and some differential equations; circuits or mechanics help but are optional.

### Course structure

The official [video lectures](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-007-signals-and-systems-spring-2011/video_galleries/video-lectures/) contain 26 sessions. Lectures 1–6 develop signals, systems, convolution, LTI properties, and differential or difference equations. Lectures 7–12 cover CT/DT Fourier series, transforms, and filtering. Lectures 13–19 treat modulation, sampling, interpolation, and the CT/DT interface. Lectures 20–24 move through Laplace and z transforms, CT-to-DT filter mapping, and Butterworth design; lectures 25–26 close with feedback and an inverted pendulum.

The [lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-007-signals-and-systems-spring-2011/pages/lecture-notes/) are not transcripts. Video is better for the motivation behind a derivation, while the notes are better for definitions and conditions. The course's strongest design choice is keeping convolution sums and integrals, CT/DT Fourier transforms, and Laplace/z transforms beside each other. Their formulas may look similar, but their independent variables, frequency periodicity, regions of convergence, and stability conditions differ.

The pace of the old videos has a particular advantage. Convolution is explained through graphical motion as well as an integral; sampling moves from replicated continuous spectra to periodic discrete frequency; and the Laplace and z transforms repeatedly use the region of convergence to connect an algebraic expression with causality and stability. Alternating the continuous and discrete viewpoints keeps sampling and filter mapping from becoming isolated topics at the end of a text.

### Every Lecture Has a Problem Set and Solution

The [assignments page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-007-signals-and-systems-spring-2011/pages/assignments/) pairs all 26 lectures, from the introduction through the inverted-pendulum example, with problems and solutions. The instructor says recommended problems should be treated as required and solutions should confirm an answer or be a last resort. When a solution follows a different route, compare assumptions, transform conventions, and results rather than rewriting the derivation to match.

There is no formal exams page, but the lecture-by-lecture sets already form an unusually continuous practice sequence. If time is limited, do not skip convolution, sampling and aliasing, regions of convergence, or CT/DT filter mapping; those topics connect the earlier and later portions of the course.

### Numerical exercise

A companion notebook can follow the same waveform: derive its Fourier transform, sample it to observe windowing and leakage, lower the sample rate to create aliasing, and check a filter through both convolution and frequency multiplication. At minimum, distinguish \(f\) from \(\omega\), Hz from rad/s, and CT from DT normalization. Otherwise a “wrong spectrum” is hard to diagnose.

The official [readings](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-007-signals-and-systems-spring-2011/pages/readings/) map to the 1982 Oppenheim–Willsky textbook; OCW does not bundle the full copyrighted book. Despite the age of the videos, this combination of continuous/discrete comparison, complete notes, and lecture-level solved problems remains unusual.
