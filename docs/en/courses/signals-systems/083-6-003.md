### Course fit

Dennis Freeman's MIT [6.003 Fall 2011](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/) is the offering recommended here. Its archive has complete notes, 14 homework sets with solutions, 3 quizzes, and a final, making it a strong choice for independent learners who want substantial written practice; it has no official programming lab or project. The 25 lectures move from discrete and continuous signals, difference and differential equations, and convolution to poles and zeros, Z/Laplace/Fourier representations, sampling, feedback, and communications applications.

HW1 is a useful starting point. If complex exponentials, geometric series, or difference equations stop the calculation, the algebra needs review. If the calculation works but signal versus system or linearity versus time invariance remains unclear, the first 3 lectures deserve a slower reading. The later difficulty is keeping initial conditions, ROC, sample rate, and units consistent when the same object moves among time, frequency, and complex-frequency representations.

### Course structure

The [Calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/calendar/) divides naturally into blocks. Lectures 1–8 establish system language before Quiz 1; 9–13 use poles, frequency response, Bode plots, and feedback before Quiz 2; 14–19 connect Fourier series, the Fourier transform, and DTFT before Quiz 3; 20–25 cover sampling, quantization, and modulation. Carrying the same second-order system through these blocks helps its equation, impulse response, system function, pole plot, and Bode plot become different views of the same object.

### Coursework

All HW1–HW14 files on [Assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/assignments/) have solutions. HW4, 7, 10, and 14 were exam practice and are best saved for the corresponding tests. The worthwhile difficulty is rarely elaborate algebra. It is understanding how poles and ROC jointly determine causality, stability, and the existence of a frequency response, and how analog frequency, sample rate, and normalized digital frequency jointly determine aliasing.

Sascha Spors's unofficial [Jupyter notebooks](https://github.com/spatialaudio/signals-and-systems-lecture) are published under CC BY 4.0 and provide computational examples for continuous- and discrete-time LTI systems, convolution, Fourier and Laplace analysis, and sampling. They work well as a visual companion after hand derivation but do not replace MIT's 14 native assignments. The central payoff of 6.003 is being able to look at a pole plot or response curve and immediately identify the initial-condition, ROC, and sampling assumptions behind it—the line between performing transforms and understanding a system.
