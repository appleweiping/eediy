### Course fit

MIT 6.5950/6.5951 **Secure Hardware Design, Spring 2025** treats
microarchitecture as a security boundary and covers cache side channels,
transient execution, Rowhammer, CPU fuzzing, formal verification, and TEEs.
It fits learners with roughly 6.004-level architecture who can debug C and
RTL. Review computer organization first if virtual addresses, cache sets, and
speculative state are not yet distinct.

Attacks belong only on personally owned or explicitly authorized devices and
data isolated from production. Without compliant bare metal, vulnerable DRAM,
or institutional supervision, Spectre, Rowhammer, ASLR, and cache-attack work
should stop at models, public traces, and defensive analysis. That boundary
matters, but the learning goal remains to connect an attack channel to a
defensive property that can be tested or proved.

### Labs and projects

The [official calendar](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/calendar.html) lists 14
lectures, moving from side channels, caches, and transient execution through
physical attacks, HSMs, Rowhammer, hardware-software contracts, CPU fuzzing,
formal verification, and TEEs. For each topic, identify the asset, attacker
capability, propagation channel, and defensive property instead of recording
only an attack-success rate.

The [labs index](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/labs.html) still says 6 exercises,
while its table lists Lab 0–7, which is 8: C debugging, website fingerprinting,
caches, Spectre, Rowhammer, ASLR bypass, RISC-V/RTL fuzzing, and formal
verification. The public
[starter repository](https://github.com/MATCHA-MIT/SHD-StarterCode)
contains Lab 0–7 templates but no solutions, grading scripts, or course
servers.

Access differs sharply. Lab 2, Lab 3, and Lab 5 depend on course bare-metal
hosts; Lab 4 needs profiled DRAM and HTCondor. The
[Lab 6 handout](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/labs/fuzz.html) also relies on
`unicorn.csail.mit.edu` and PSP RTL simulation. By contrast, the
[Lab 7 handout](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/labs/formal.html) explicitly
supports local Docker. A practical sequence is Lab 0, a controlled Lab 1, and
Lab 7, using the remaining labs to study threat models, interfaces, and
defensive properties.

### MIT and UNSW Are Different Paths

MIT emphasizes CPU caches, Spectre, Rowhammer, fuzzing, and formal methods.
For cryptographic implementations, power, and fault injection, UNSW COMP6420
is more direct. Its [syllabus](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~cs6420/syllabus/)
and [Hackster platform description](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~cs6420/hackster/)
document an iCE40 FPGA, two RP2040 devices, a high-speed ADC, 5 labs, and a
CTF. Choose by research question and available equipment, not by course name.

### One Defect Can Compare Two Defenses

A safe outside project plants one documented arithmetic or permission defect
in a self-authored small RISC-V/RTL block and compares differential fuzzing
with formal assertions in simulation only. Preserve the trigger, minimized
counterexample, and waveform; after the fix, require both regressions to pass
and explain what each method found or missed. This is not a port of MIT Lab 6,
but it joins a threat model, dynamic search, formal property, and mitigation around
one defect.
