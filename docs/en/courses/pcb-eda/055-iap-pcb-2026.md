### Course structure

MIT's [The Art and Science of PCB Design](https://pcb.mit.edu/) fits learners who know basic circuits and want one complete board-design chain. The current page describes 6.S092/EC.S03 IAP 2026: four weeks, 8 KiCad labs, and one radio throughout. The board contains an ESP32, display, dual audio amplification, USB and battery power, and an FM front end. Many PCB tutorials teach schematic capture or routing in isolation. Here, an early current budget returns as a constraint on return paths, a footprint returns at assembly, and first power-up interrogates decisions made weeks earlier.

The official [syllabus](https://pcb.mit.edu/syllabus/) recommends basic R/L/C, op-amp, and power analysis. “All experience levels” should not be read as no prerequisites. [Lab 02](https://pcb.mit.edu/labs/lab_02/) is the honest readiness test: design around an AD8592 with a 10 kΩ source, 80 mVrms maximum input, 8 Ω load, 200 mA peak current, and a 500 Hz–10 kHz band. If the 5 V supply, output-current limit, stability, and coupling-capacitor corner cannot be recovered from the datasheet, op-amps, RC networks, and SPICE deserve attention before KiCad.

### Labs and projects

The [2026 Schedule](https://pcb.mit.edu/schedule/) moves from requirements and a block diagram to an LTspice amplifier, hierarchical schematic, matched symbols and footprints, layout and return paths, a purchasable BOM, ERC and DRC, Gerber and NC drill output, assembly, and power-domain bring-up. Treating each lab as an isolated exercise defeats the design: the Lab 02 gain, capacitor, and current assumptions disappear during layout, clipping appears after assembly, and a part gets changed by instinct.

A better reading follows one audio path. Simulation predicts gain and band; the schematic defines source and load; layout separates small-signal and high-current returns; assembly brings bandwidth, noise, and clipping back for measurement. When they disagree, walk backward through model, source impedance, loudspeaker equivalent, and probe connection. The PCB then remains circuit design rather than becoming an attractive routing picture.

[KiBot](https://github.com/INTI-CMNB/KiBot) is an active AGPL-3.0 KiCad automation tool. A pinned configuration can regenerate BOM, Gerbers, drill data, assembly drawings, and check reports, making it useful for export diffs and CI. Layers, holes, outline, and coordinates still deserve inspection in a native viewer. Automation catches drift; it does not supply judgment.

Before taking on the radio, the smaller [TMP117 two-layer-board exercise (not an official MIT lab)](https://github.com/appleweiping/eediy/tree/main/examples/tmp117-kicad) shows how KiCad sources become manufacturing outputs. It has not been validated by assembling a physical board, so use it to inspect the relationship among schematic, layout, and exports—not as an order-ready design.

### Access and version notes

[Lab 07](https://pcb.mit.edu/labs/lab_07/) says that staff predesigned and manufactured an electrically similar course board to meet the four-week logistics. Assembling the staff board demonstrates assembly and bring-up, not successful fabrication of a student's layout. Sending personal Gerbers to a board house does not provide the staff BOM, stencil, or validated firmware. Lab 08 also points to an MIT-internal GitHub repository for ESP32 firmware, so an outside learner should treat firmware as new work when access is unavailable.

Both outside stopping points are respectable. The design route ends after human inspection of schematic, PCB, BOM, Gerbers, and drill data, plus a fabrication quote. The physical route accepts procurement, DFM, firmware, and the possibility that a personal first spin fails. Some recordings require MIT authentication; the 2026 slides and lab writeups are the dependable spine, rather than unmarked video from another year.

### First power-up is more revealing than the radio playing

Before fabrication, be able to explain the stackup, library and footprint provenance, substitutions, ERC and DRC results, and every layer and hole in a Gerber viewer. On physical hardware, begin with current limiting, energize one domain at a time, inspect 3.3 V and 5 V rails and abnormal heating, then approach audio, buttons, display, and reception. USB and battery exclusion cannot rest on “it should be fine.”

Hot air, solder paste, and rework require eye protection, extraction, a heat-safe area, and training. When those are unavailable, manufacturing-ready files are a sensible finish. The course has landed when the learner can name the assumption that broke in SPICE, layout, or first power-up—and the exact board change it would cause next time.
