## Course focus

MIT [18.02SC](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/) suits learners who know single-variable calculus but do not yet trust their spatial reasoning. Denis Auroux's Scholar edition includes lectures, recitations, problem sets, and exams. The official [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/pages/syllabus/) lists 18.01 as the prerequisite and estimates roughly 150 hours for full participation. The new burden is keeping object, coordinates, and orientation consistent: scalar or vector field, curve or surface, chosen normal, and induced boundary direction. If the major integral theorems are already fluent and the goal is \(n\)-dimensional space and differential forms, consider the more abstract 18.022 instead.

## Course structure

The sequence begins with vectors, matrices, planes, and parametric curves; moves through gradients, directional derivatives, and Lagrange multipliers; then reaches double integrals, line integrals, and Green's theorem before finishing with triple integrals, surface flux, Gauss, and Stokes. For every new integral, draw the region and state parameter ranges, differential element, orientation, and units. A move to polar, cylindrical, or spherical coordinates must transform the region, boundary, and Jacobian together. Even a lone sign error should be traced to a particular normal or traversal.

The cleanest interface to electromagnetics appears in Unit 4's [Physics Applications](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/pages/4.-triple-integrals-and-surface-integrals-in-3-space/physics-applications/), where “Curl and Physics” is followed by “Maxwell's Equations.” Do not begin by memorizing four field equations. First be able to name the region, boundary, orientation, and theorem that converts an integral form to a differential one.

Keep a two-column record for the major integral theorems. The left column holds a local quantity such as divergence or curl; the right holds measurable flux or circulation over a region and its boundary. Draw the positive orientation, state smoothness or simple-connectivity conditions, and keep one counterexample where a condition fails. Green, Gauss, and Stokes then become related statements rather than separate formula sheets. A single cylindrical region also makes a good coordinate check: Cartesian and cylindrical calculations should agree in value, volume units, and boundary orientation.

## Assignments and feedback

Choose an analytic vector field and a rectangular or cylindrical region. Calculate divergence, curl, boundary integrals, and region integrals, then deliberately reverse the normal and explain the sign change. Keep the sketch, parameterization, theorem conditions, and units beside the result; a numerical match alone is not the artifact. Add numerical quadrature on successively finer grids and check that the discretization error converges. Record the mesh, quadrature rule, boundary discretization, and original parameters so the calculation can be rerun exactly. The analytic identity establishes boundary and orientation; the numerical result only checks the implementation.

The official [Exam 2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/pages/2.-partial-derivatives/exam-2/) and three-hour [final exam](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/pages/final-exam-1/) both provide papers and solutions. Attempt them under time limits, then classify misses as object, coordinate, orientation, theorem-condition, or calculation errors. The habit worth carrying into electromagnetics is simple: draw the boundary, set the orientation, and only then integrate.
