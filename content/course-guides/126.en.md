## Access and course scope

Choose MIT [6.152J Fall 2005](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/pages/syllabus/) for fabrication-process reasoning when device cross-sections and basic rate equations are already readable. It places diffusion, oxidation, lithography, CVD and PVD, etching, metallization, vacuum systems, CMOS, and advanced silicon processing in one theory sequence. It also schedules a 4-hour lab each week for 9 weeks, during which students fabricate MOS capacitors, microcantilevers, and microfluidic devices. Public self-study can reproduce theory, process-flow reasoning, problems, and report analysis—not cleanroom fabrication. The syllabus says instructor permission rather than “no prerequisite.”

## One process flow connects the theory

The official [Lecture Notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/resources/lecture-notes/) have no lecture video. The useful notebook is not a glossary but one evolving cross-section and flow: how oxidation and diffusion alter material; how CVD and PVD introduce coverage, stress, contamination, and thermal-budget consequences; how lithography transfers layout into resist and material; what selectivity and anisotropy mean in wet, dry, and KOH etching; and how the steps combine into a MOS, MEMS, or microfluidic device. At every step, record critical parameters, measurable quantities, alignment, contamination, and undercut failures, and compatibility with the next operation.

The [Assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/resources/assignments/) page publishes only PS1–PS8 (8 problem sets), all with solutions. The final 2 homework items named in the syllabus came from attendance and questions in later lectures and are not downloadable problem sets, so “all 10 are public” is wrong. [Exams](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/resources/exams/) preserves a take-home paper, a Quiz 1 solution, and a Quiz 2 example from other terms, not a complete one-to-one Fall 2005 assessment set.

## Laboratory sequences and the cleanroom boundary

The [Labs](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/pages/labs/) page shows 3 sequences: gate oxide and poly, backside etch, and MOS-capacitor test for IC; photolithography, KOH etch, and cantilever test for MEMS; and photolithography, molding, and microfluidic test for Fluids. What is public is the process and report expectation, not a safety manual authorizing off-campus equipment use. The IC report was first returned for writing revision and then assessed for technical content, making clear that explaining process, data, and failure is itself an outcome.

Without a cleanroom, prepare a pre-fabrication report for one device: full flow, versioned cross-sections, materials and thermal budget, parameter sweep, expected measurements, failure modes, and public-data provenance. Label it process planning or simulation, not completion of an MIT lab.

## A public self-study endpoint

Use the [Laboratory Safety guide](../../guides/safety.md) for general
cleanroom stop conditions and chemical, vacuum, and high-temperature
boundaries, and the [Reproducibility guide](../../guides/reproducibility.md)
for version, parameter, and evidence records. Returning to MIT's official
[6.152J archive](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/),
the course-specific judgment is narrower: the 2005 lecture/lab flow explains how oxidation, diffusion,
deposition, lithography, etching, and testing form a MOS capacitor,
cantilever, or microfluidic device. It is not a 2026 recipe and grants no MIT
cleanroom access.

The public exit is a manufacturing plan: explain why each step exists, what
surface and thermal history it inherits, how the next step can fail, and
which electrical, mechanical, or fluidic measurement separates plausible
causes. A complete flow, released problems, and an honest pre-fabrication
report cover the outside material. Without a qualified facility, do not call
the result a fabricated device.

The central idea in 6.152J is process memory: oxidation, implantation,
annealing, and etching leave surfaces and thermal history that constrain every
later device step rather than erasing that history.
