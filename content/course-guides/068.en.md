## Current EE263 is a different course

Since Fall 2025, [Stanford EE263](https://ee263.stanford.edu/) has become
*Matrix Methods: Singular Value Decomposition*, while EE363 carries the former
linear-dynamical-systems thread. Current EE263 covers range and null spaces,
QR, least squares and least norm, eigenvalues, SVD, Gaussian models, and MMSE,
with Engr 108/Math 104-level linear algebra and basic probability as
prerequisites. For controllability, observability, and state-space dynamics,
choose the 2008 archive directly rather than attaching old videos to current
assignments.

## Current materials and access

Summer 2026 is in person and recordings live in Canvas. As checked on
2026-07-29, [Lectures](https://ee263.stanford.edu/lectures.html) publishes
through slide set 12; the complete 30-set Fall 2025 sequence extends through
recursive estimation, Gauss–Newton, Gaussian/MMSE, and spectral graph
embedding.

[Homework](https://ee263.stanford.edu/homework.html) uses Julia and JSON data,
with HW1–HW5 currently public. [Exams](https://ee263.stanford.edu/exams.html)
gives an approximate policy of homework 25%, midterm 25%, and final 50%, but
Canvas, Gradescope, recordings, and feedback are not anonymously open.

[CVXPY](https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy) can independently check bounded least
squares or KKT conditions after the derivation, but it is not the assigned
Julia environment for current EE263. Keep the same \(A,b\) and compare the
hand derivation, Julia work, and CVXPY result by residual, constraints, and
dual interpretation. A Python rewrite should not be presented as course
homework.

## The 2008 linear dynamical systems archive

[Stanford Engineering Everywhere](https://see.stanford.edu/Course/EE263)
collects 20 videos and transcripts, 9 homework sets, a midterm, final, and
MATLAB data; the accompanying archive adds lecture slides, support notes, and
a 414-page course reader. This version expects linear algebra, differential
equations, Laplace transforms, and transfer functions, and moves from least
squares and matrix exponentials through state-space response, SVD,
controllability, and observability.

One physical system can carry the route: derive \(A,B,C,D\) from its ODE,
compare free and forced response, change state coordinates while checking
input-output behavior, and give a physical meaning to an unreachable or
unobservable mode. When porting MATLAB data to Python or Julia, preserve
matrix dimensions, tolerances, and an original reference result. The homework
15%, take-home midterm 40%, and take-home final 45% on the historical
[2007–08 course-information page](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/ee/ee263/ee263.1082/courseinfo.html)
belong only to that version.

Choose current Matrix Methods for SVD/MMSE and Julia; choose the 2008 archive
for an LDS course with public videos, problems, exams, and data. Label notes
with the version so the shared course number does not imply a false
continuity.
