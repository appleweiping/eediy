## Fix the Transform Convention before Reading the Geometry

Stanford SEE [EE261](https://see.stanford.edu/Course/EE261), Brad Osgood's
Fall 2007 course, moves from Fourier series and transforms through
distributions, sampling, the DFT/FFT, 2D transforms, and Radon tomography. The
[syllabus](https://see.stanford.edu/materials/lsoftaee261/Syllabus.pdf) frames
the goal as recognizing when, why, and how to use a transform. It fits
learners with calculus, complex exponentials, and basic signals background who
want a shared language for Shah/Poisson, diffraction, lattices, and imaging.
Use an engineering introduction first for initial LTI, Laplace/z-transform,
or filter-implementation study.

## Course structure

The representation lens connects orthogonality, duality, scaling, and
convolution. The distribution lens shows how delta and Shah work where
ordinary functions fail. The sampling lens moves from Poisson summation to
spectrum replication, aliasing, and finite-record leakage. The geometry lens
connects rotation, lattices, and the projection-slice theorem to tomography.
All derivations share a convention sheet for exponent sign, \(2\pi\),
frequency units, sinc/Shah definitions, DFT normalization, and 2D determinant
scaling.

[scikit-image](https://github.com/scikit-image/scikit-image) is a
BSD-3-Clause image-processing library whose repository includes Radon
transform and reconstruction examples. It is neither EE261 starter code nor a
solution set. Rebuild a sinogram from a simple phantom, vary projection-angle
count and image resolution, and check coordinate direction, filter
normalization, and reconstruction error against the projection-slice theorem.

For the exit, choose 2 applications: sampling/audio and another from
diffraction, crystallography, or tomography. Begin from conventions and the
central derivation, validate with course data or shareable input, and apply a
reverse check through Parseval, units, or an inverse transform. A plot that
cannot explain normalization, coordinate change, and approximation is not yet
an EE261-style Fourier argument.

## Boundary with Engineering Signals and DSP Courses

[MIT 6.003](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/)
is better for continuous/discrete LTI systems, differential/difference
equations, and Laplace/z-transforms.
[MIT RES.6-008](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-008-digital-signal-processing-spring-2011/)
emphasizes IIR/FIR structures, coefficient quantization, and realizable
implementation. EE261's strength is mathematical and geometric transfer of
Fourier ideas; it does not replace a full DSP implementation course.
Whichever software you use, round-trip a Gaussian or rectangular input and
check amplitude, units, and coordinate direction before trusting a more
complicated imaging result.
