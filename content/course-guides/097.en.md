## Build a normalization ledger on page 1

The common failure in EE261 is not inability to integrate; it is joining correct formulas computed under different conventions. Before starting, choose and record the sign and \(2\pi\) placement for the continuous Fourier transform, whether frequency is \(f\) or \(\omega\), definitions of rect, sinc, and Shah/III, placement of \(1/N\) in the DFT pair, and coordinate and determinant scaling for the 2D transform. Return to this ledger before every scaling, duality, Poisson summation, or sampling step instead of trying to reconstruct constants from memory. It is not merely a formula sheet: record units and domains, since time scaling changes both spectral width and amplitude and a 2D coordinate transform adds an area factor. When switching books, software functions, or solution conventions, run a Gaussian or rectangle through a forward/inverse round trip before continuing.

The Stanford Engineering Everywhere [EE261 home](https://see.stanford.edu/Course/EE261) gives Brad Osgood's explicit goal: not just calculating a transform, but recognizing when, why, and how to use it. The public [Syllabus and Schedule](https://see.stanford.edu/materials/lsoftaee261/Syllabus.pdf) is marked Fall 2007 and calls its schedule an approximation; the course runs from Fourier series to the Radon transform and medical imaging. The normalization ledger is an EEDIY supplement, not a Stanford handout.

## Use 4 lenses instead of chapter summaries

The first is the representation lens. Lectures 1–10 move from periodicity, orthogonality, and Fourier series into the continuous transform, duality, scaling, convolution, and the central limit theorem. Write both analysis and synthesis for every result and perform 1 reverse check using Parseval or a dimensional relation. The second is the distribution lens. Lectures 11–16 use rapidly decreasing test functions, tempered distributions, delta, and Shah where ordinary functions fail, then connect diffraction and crystallography.

The third is the sampling lens. Lectures 17–22 move from interpolation and aliasing into the DFT and FFT. Do not reduce sampling to \(f_s>2f_{\max}\). State spectral replication, the anti-alias assumption, finite records, periodic indexing, and the fact that ideal formulas do not remove window and leakage effects. The fourth is the operator-and-geometry lens. Lectures 23–30 move from linear systems, impulse response, and LTI eigenfunctions into 2D transforms, rotation and scaling, lattices, tomography, and Radon inversion.

These 4 lenses deliberately cross textbook chapters. One delta is both a distribution and a sampler; one reciprocal relation explains time-frequency scaling and crystal lattices; one projection-slice relation connects a 2D spectrum to tomography. After a lecture group, draw an object–operation–domain–application map instead of only listing transform pairs.

## Five blackboard diagnostics are more credible than video progress

Diagnostic 1: derive rect \(\leftrightarrow\) sinc under the chosen convention and check width and amplitude by scaling. Diagnostic 2: manipulate delta, unit step, and Shah as distributions, stating which products or convolutions are not automatically defined. Diagnostic 3: derive sampling spectrum replication from Poisson summation and construct 2 continuous sinusoids that produce identical samples at one sampling rate.

Diagnostic 4: calculate a small-\(N\) DFT by hand, explaining periodic indexing, circular shift, and FFT factorization. Diagnostic 5: prove how the 2D Fourier transform changes under rotation and use the projection-slice theorem to explain frequency slices in Radon data. A failure on item 1 calls for calculus and complex exponentials; item 2 returns to Lectures 11–14; items 3–4 return to Lectures 17–22; item 5 returns to Lectures 26–30.

These are EEDIY placement diagnostics, not Stanford exams. Complete at least 4 without a solution reference, including definitions, derivations, and a convention audit. Calling FFT or displaying an image without explaining normalization and coordinate change is not enough to mark the course complete.

## Treat the MATLAB files as a 2007 archive

The home page says, “Some homework problem may require the Sinesum2 Matlab Software, see Software below,” but the public page body reviewed on 2026-07-29 does not display the corresponding Software section or a Sinesum2 download. That is an access gap, not permission to invent a current installation procedure. The PS5 `.txt` MATLAB files, audio, GIF, ZIP, and images remain linked in assignment rows. Preserve the original file and hash before compatibility work.

EEDIY recommends porting required computation to current MATLAB/Octave or Python with NumPy/SciPy while retaining a compatibility note: original filename, read assumptions, sampling metadata, function or API changes, and output differences. A port is not an official Stanford update. For anti-aliasing audio and image filtering in particular, preserve source input, filter response, boundary handling, clipping or normalization, and before/after artifacts instead of submitting one polished image.

## It is neither a first signals course nor an implementation DSP course

MIT [6.003 Signals and Systems](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/) is a better first engineering spine for continuous and discrete LTI systems, convolution, difference and differential equations, Laplace and z-transforms, causality, and system response. EE261 expects the learner to tolerate more mathematical Fourier language and connects distributions, Poisson summation, diffraction, lattices, and the Radon transform. It does not fill gaps in ROC analysis, state-space representation, or control-oriented modeling.

MIT [RES.6-008 Digital Signal Processing](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-008-digital-signal-processing-spring-2011/) turns z-transforms, digital network structures, IIR/FIR design, coefficient quantization, and FFT into a 20-lesson implementation spine and supplies solution packets for Lectures 2–20. Choose EE261 to see why Fourier ideas cross mathematics, optics, and imaging. Choose RES.6-008 to design realizable discrete filters. If LTI foundations are weak, begin with 6.003. EE261's DFT/FFT segment does not replace a full DSP implementation course.
