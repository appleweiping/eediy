## Start with the Boundaries of the Fall 2011 Release

This guide selects Dennis Freeman’s Fall 2011 MIT 6.003 instead of combining
Signals and Systems material from several years under a versionless “classic
course.” The [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/)
defines its objects precisely: discrete- and continuous-time signals,
difference and differential equations, convolution, poles and zeros,
frequency response, Z/Laplace/Fourier representations, sampling, and
applications in feedback, communications, and signal processing. The original
grade used three midterms worth 10%, 15%, and 20%, a 40% final, and 15% for
homework, participation, and other factors. An independent learner need not
copy those weights. The papers are still valuable because they reveal whether
a derivation can be completed without prompts.

The public release forms an unusually complete theory loop: 25 videos with
matching notes, 14 assignments with solutions, the three Fall 2011 quizzes and
final, and most corresponding exams from Fall 2009 and Spring 2010. There is
no official programming laboratory or course project, and the site prescribes
no MATLAB or Python toolchain. A notebook added for self-study should not be
misrepresented as a 6.003 lab.

## Begin with HW1, Not a Generic Prerequisite Checklist

The official prerequisite is named only as 6.02, which is not an actionable
description for an outside learner. Print HW1 and work on it before watching a
lecture or opening a solution. Stop only where the mathematics or a definition
actually blocks progress. Difficulty manipulating complex exponentials,
geometric series, or difference equations identifies a concrete prerequisite
to revisit. If the algebra proceeds but “signal,” “system,” “linear,” or
“time-invariant” remains vague, return to the definitions and examples in
Lectures 1–3. Keep this first HW1 draft; its mistakes are useful when the set
is corrected later.

After Lectures 1–8, take the Fall 2011 Quiz 1 using the duration printed on
that exam PDF rather than imposing one generic time limit. Locate each lost
point in an action: forming a difference equation, handling an initial
condition, drawing poles, or changing representations. For later
Z/Laplace/Fourier work, write the signal class, ROC, initial conditions, and
sample rate beside the derivation. Much of 6.003's difficulty comes from
dropping an assumption while changing domains, not from a difficult integral.

## Follow the Original Exam Boundaries through Twenty-Five Lectures

The [calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/calendar/)
places all 25 meetings, homework, and exams on one timeline. I would preserve
its exam boundaries instead of dividing the videos evenly by week:

1. **Lectures 1–8: system language.** Work through difference/differential
   equations, feedback, poles, Z and Laplace transforms, discrete
   approximation of continuous systems, and convolution. Take Quiz 1 closed
   book, then classify every miss as algebra, representation conversion, or
   an omitted assumption.
2. **Lectures 9–13: frequency response and control.** Move from discrete
   frequency response to continuous Bode plots and feedback stability.
   Describe one second-order system with an impulse response, system
   function, pole plot, and Bode plot before taking Quiz 2.
3. **Lectures 14–19: the Fourier family.** Cover Fourier series, Fourier
   transforms, DTFT, and their relations. Do not stop at a transform table:
   explain how periodization, sampling, and spectral replication generate one
   representation from another, then take Quiz 3.
4. **Lectures 20–25: applications, sampling, quantization, and modulation.**
   Apply frequency-domain tools to filtering or imaging, sampling, and AM/FM,
   then use the Fall 2011 final as a timed whole-course test.

The [lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/lecture-notes/)
match the 25 video titles. Read a note and write predictions first, use the
video to correct them, and redraw the important plots without either source.
Playing 25 videos consecutively creates familiarity while hiding sign, ROC,
and sample-scale errors.

## Treat the Fourteen Assignments as Learning Sets and Mock Exams

The [assignments page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/assignments/)
publishes HW1–HW14 with all solutions. HW4, HW7, HW10, and HW14 were practice
for the three quizzes and final and were neither collected nor graded. For
independent study, solve the other ten first and save an uncorrected draft
before opening a solution. Keep that draft unchanged, then reserve the four
practice sets for a timed,
closed-note sitting immediately before their associated exams. HW5 and HW11
include image data; store original data separately from solutions so that
the answer is not revealed accidentally.

For each problem, retain the original derivation, a unit or boundary check,
an error category, and the reason for the revision. The official
[exams page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/pages/exams/)
states that Spring 2010 Quiz 3 was omitted because it covered different
topics. Therefore, exams across years are not interchangeable. Use Fall 2011
as the primary measurement and Fall 2009 and Spring 2010 only as transfer
tests. Use the duration printed on each paper, and write missing papers and
coverage differences beside each score.

## Label the Self-Study Project as an EEDIY Extension

By the end, the course directory should contain four transform-domain comparison
sheets, first and corrected submissions for ten learning assignments, four
timed exams, and an indexed error log. As an EEDIY extension—not an MIT
project—also produce a compact LTI identification report. Define a known,
stable system in Python, Julia, or Octave and recover it from impulse
response, swept-frequency, and ordinary input-output evidence. Compare
time-domain convolution with frequency-domain multiplication, then introduce
a finite record, a signal near Nyquist, and saturation to measure where the
model fails.

The report should include the system equation, sample rate, raw inputs and
outputs, reproducible code, pole-zero and Bode plots, a residual table, and
one page about failure cases. For a noiseless baseline, predeclare the
normalized test `||y_hat-y||_2 / ||y||_2 < 0.01`; exclude a zero-energy
reference output or define its denominator separately. This 1% threshold is
an EEDIY project specification, not an official course criterion. A learner
without programming experience can use a low-order hand-computed system and
make the same cross-check. The goal is agreement among representations, not
familiarity with a software interface.

## Cost, Tools, and the Public Boundary

This route can remain entirely in software. It needs no oscilloscope, DAQ, or
signal generator and introduces no electrical hazard. The official course
does not specify a programming environment; open-source Python/NumPy, Julia,
or Octave is an editorial choice. Connecting a sound card or laboratory
instrument later requires a new check of input amplitude, grounding, sampling
rate, and device protection. Simulation safety cannot simply be extended to
hardware.

Every linked course artifact here belongs to Fall 2011. The second edition of
*Signals and Systems* is the textbook named by the syllabus, but that does not
make the complete copyrighted book an OCW download. The public videos, notes,
assignments, and exams are enough for this route; use only a legally obtained
copy of the text as a supplement. Completion should mean passing four timed
exams, correcting ten assignments, and reproducing the cross-check among
representations—not reaching the end of a video playlist.
