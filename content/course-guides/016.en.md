## Course fit

[MIT 6.087 Practical Programming in C](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-087-practical-programming-in-c-january-iap-2010/) is a three-week January IAP 2010 course: 5 lectures of 1.5 hours and a 3-hour lab each week. It fits learners who already understand loops, functional decomposition, and debugging but have not studied C systematically. [Assignment 1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-087-practical-programming-in-c-january-iap-2010/pages/assignments/) is the quickest starting point. If command-line compilation and call stacks are both unfamiliar, a general programming introduction is a better first course.

## Course materials

The [Lecture Notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-087-practical-programming-in-c-january-iap-2010/pages/lecture-notes/) contain 14 slide sets, moving from types, pointers, I/O, structures, and dynamic allocation to data structures, function pointers, libraries, threads, sockets, and Linux IPC. That is broad coverage for 3 weeks, so the later topics are introductions. The ideas that recur throughout the course are object lifetime, memory ownership, error paths, and partial I/O; they connect the early pointer exercises to the later socket material.

The assignments are numbered 1–7, although the page has 8 rows because 6a and 6b are parts of Assignment 6. They move from small pointer and data-structure exercises toward system interfaces. A successful run on ordinary input is the easy part. The harder questions are whether a caller knows who invokes `free`, how a truncated read terminates, and whether warnings, stderr, and exit status agree with the interface. Strict warnings and sanitizers in current GCC or Clang are useful aids, but they were not part of the 2010 course environment.

## Labs and projects

On the official [Labs](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-087-practical-programming-in-c-january-iap-2010/pages/labs/) page, Lab 1 Game of Life includes starter code and a solution, with boundary cells, still lifes, and oscillators supplying the interesting cases. Lab 2 Huffman has no public solution and compresses trees, byte representation, and bit I/O into the same program. Empty files, single-symbol files, and truncated bitstreams quickly reveal whether an implementation understands the format or merely passes the sample.

Before Lab 2, the [fixed-capacity ring-buffer exercise (not an official MIT lab)](https://github.com/appleweiping/eediy/tree/main/examples/ring-buffer) offers a small place to watch ownership fail at empty, full, and wraparound boundaries. It is only a bridge into the larger pointer and dynamic-memory problems in Huffman.

The [Final Project brief](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-087-practical-programming-in-c-january-iap-2010/9796fe2dda5404aa9d14a49081b842d8_MIT6_087IAP10_project.pdf) asks for an open-source library, a proposal, code, and a report. OpenCV, FFTW, and SQLite were suggestions in 2010 rather than a current fixed list. OCW does not provide public access to the course's Athena environment, and the [current Athena service page](https://ist.mit.edu/athena) limits the service to MIT community members with the relevant account and network access. Modern Linux, WSL, or a container is therefore the practical route for most independent learners. If only 1 task is chosen to capture the course's character, Lab 2 Huffman comes much closer to “Practical Programming” than memorizing C syntax.
