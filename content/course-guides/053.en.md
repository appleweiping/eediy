## The First Gate Is Authorization, Not Installation

These assignments concern channels that can affect real devices and other users. Work only on devices and data that are personally owned or covered by explicit written authorization and isolated from production. Do not collect third-party browsing histories, recover real keys, disrupt a shared server, bypass access control, or disable mitigations on a production host. Website fingerprinting must use self-authored pages and controlled local tabs. Stop Spectre, Rowhammer, ASLR, and cache work at modeling and defensive analysis when no compliant laboratory exists. Technical feasibility is not legal or institutional authorization.

That boundary determines every later branch. Publicly reproducible work proceeds to code and verification; work requiring special bare metal or vulnerable DRAM stops at handouts, traces, and defensive models. Record the authorized object, device owner, isolation method, permitted effect, and stop condition before starting an experiment. Without those fields, an apparently complete environment still must not run an attack workload.

## Then Pin the Course Contract: Spring 2025

MIT 6.5950/6.5951, *Secure Hardware Design*, is not a course about merely moving cryptographic algorithms into RTL. It treats microarchitecture as a security boundary and studies cache side channels, transient execution, Rowhammer, hardware-software contracts, fuzzing, formal verification, and TEEs. The MIT OCW course home identifies this as Spring 2025. It targets advanced undergraduates, MEng students, and PhD students with 6.004-level computer architecture and C debugging; the institutional format is 12 units, 3-0-9.

Put the version in every study record. Spring 2025 had no midterm or final and centered assessment on 8 labs. For graduate students, labs accounted for 85%, paper discussion for 12%, and random in-person checkoff for 3%; for undergraduates, labs were 97% and checkoff 3%. A cumulative exam introduced in a later offering must not be projected backward, and the 6.5950/6.5951 distinction must not be misrepresented as two public courses. This is R0 desk research and makes no claim of completing institutional checkoffs, hidden tests, or special-machine work.

## What Public Material Can Reconstruct

The [official calendar](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/calendar.html) lays out 14 lectures: secure-design overview, side channels, caches, transient execution, hardware-software contracts and mitigations, then physical attacks, HSMs, Rowhammer, hardware support for software security, CPU fuzzing, formal verification, and TEEs. Its logic is not “learn attacks first and discuss defense in the final week.” Contracts, isolation, detection, and verification should follow the corresponding attack model immediately. For each topic, keep four fields: asset, attacker capability, leakage or fault channel, and a testable defensive property.

The official readings page places papers beside lectures and can restore much of the classroom argument, although several entries require an MIT Library login. Before extracting a result, record the paper's threat model, evaluation platform, controls, and conditions that prevent generalization. One attack-success number is not a substitute for those boundaries. Panopto video, Piazza, Gradescope, GitHub Classroom, and staff servers are also institutional channels, so public pages recover what to read and attempt, not critique, automated grading, or live questioning.

## Lab 0–7 Means 8 Labs, Not 6

The introduction on the [official labs index](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/labs.html) still says 6 laboratory exercises, but its own table lists Lab 0 through Lab 7, which is 8; planning should follow the itemized table. Lab 0 (5%) debugs a C note library with ASAN, UBSAN, or Valgrind. Lab 1 (10%) collects JavaScript traces on controlled pages for website fingerprinting. Lab 2 (20%) studies cache mapping, a covert channel, and two cache-attack families. Lab 3 (15%) examines the privilege boundary in Spectre. Lab 4 (15%) connects address mapping, real DRAM bit flips, and a Hamming(22,16) mitigation. Lab 5 (10%) studies ASLR bypasses. Lab 6 (15%) uses C, RISC-V/RTL, and Python for differential fuzzing of a modified Pretty Secure Processor. Lab 7 (10%) uses Yosys and Rosette to find arithmetic defects and a backdoor in that processor.

The [official starter repository](https://github.com/CSAIL-Arch-Sec/SHD-StarterCode) publicly provides templates for Lab 0–7 under the MIT License, while solutions, the gradebook, and grading scripts require contact with the course team. Lab 2, Lab 3, and Lab 5 depend on assigned bare-metal arch-sec hosts; Lab 4 additionally needs pre-profiled Rowhammer-vulnerable DRAM and HTCondor. Making a substitute program run in a VM does not reproduce the original physical assumptions or checkoff.

Lab 6 is a separate access trap. Its [official handout](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/labs/fuzz.html) and starter code can be read publicly, but the original experiment tells students to log in to `unicorn.csail.mit.edu` with course-assigned credentials and sends programs to the course's cycle-accurate RTL simulation of the modified Pretty Secure Processor. An ordinary part runs as `./run.sh partX`; this command does not require a debug port. Only the optional `./run.sh partX --debug` path additionally requires a staff-issued `SHD_DEBUG_PORT`. Thus an independent learner can audit the handout and starter code but cannot execute the original Lab 6 without the course Unicorn and PSP/RTL infrastructure; the debug port is not a general prerequisite. In contrast, the [Lab 7 handout](https://shd.mit.edu/2025/labs/formal.html) explicitly supports a local Docker environment as an alternative to Unicorn. “Public repository” therefore describes source visibility, not uniform runnability across all eight labs.

### The Gate Before Lab 0

1. Write a small C program that steps across cache lines, find one undefined behavior with a sanitizer and debugger, and explain why the failure is not a compiler behaving randomly.
2. Given a virtual address, page size, cache-set count, and associativity, explain its path through the page table, tag, index, and offset; then distinguish architectural state from microarchitectural state left by speculation.
3. Read a short RISC-V or SystemVerilog module and write one assertion plus one counterexample test for post-reset state, legal opcodes, or an addition result.

If task 1 is not reproducible, review the C memory model, sanitizers, and GDB. If task 2 produces vocabulary without a mapping, return to computer organization, caches, and virtual memory. If task 3 does not reveal sequential state, first simulate a small RTL design and inspect waveforms. After all three, proceed through Lab 0; Lecture 1–6 with controlled Lab 1; paired attack-and-defense reading; a Lab 6 handout/starter-code audit; and the locally runnable portion of Lab 7. Without an isolated bare-metal host, treat Lab 2–5 as handout analysis, public-trace study, or self-authored simulation, and do not report that reduced path as completion of the original labs.

## A Public Completion Claim Must Be Tiered

Completion evidence must be tiered honestly. Under public access, a learner can submit code, pinned tools, tests, and waveforms for Lab 0 and the local-Docker portion of Lab 7, plus a Lab 1 report using synthetic or personally controlled data. Lab 6 contributes only a sourced handout/starter-code audit unless the learner actually has course-issued Unicorn credentials and access to the course PSP/RTL simulation infrastructure; a staff-issued debug port is additionally needed only if that learner chooses `--debug`, not for an ordinary run or as a completion prerequisite. A locally invented fuzzer is a separate EEDIY exercise. For Lab 2–5, the defensible record is a threat model, handout derivation, and simulation cross-check. Unavailable evidence includes Panopto viewing records, Piazza discussion, Gradescope scores, staff hidden tests, random checkoff, course accounts, special servers, and official solutions. Listing these gaps in the README is stronger than hiding them behind “all labs completed.”

## Choose UNSW When the Target Is Physical Side Channels

The [UNSW COMP6420 syllabus](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~cs6420/syllabus/) takes a different hardware-security route. Its [public Hackster platform description](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~cs6420/hackster/) documents a bespoke board with an iCE40 FPGA, an RP2040 application microcontroller running MicroPython, a high-speed ADC that measures FPGA power, and a second RP2040 used as programmer. That platform supports scan chains, power analysis, fault injection, reverse engineering, logic locking, and PUFs; the course's public tooling emphasizes OSS-CAD/Yosys, with 5 labs and a CTF. Prefer MIT for CPU caches, Spectre, Rowhammer, fuzzing, and formal verification. Choose UNSW for cryptographic implementations and physical side channels only when compliant boards, instruments, and supervision are available. Neither course authorizes testing third-party devices.

## Let the Final Artifact Compare Two Defensive Methods

The EEDIY supplement is “fuzzing versus formal verification on a self-authored small RISC-V/RTL block,” not an official MIT project. Its fuzzer is an EEDIY-authored substitute for the inaccessible Lab 6 execution path, not a port or completion of Lab 6. In simulation only, seed one documented arithmetic or permission bug. Build a differential fuzzer on one side and a formal assertion set on the other; preserve triggering inputs, minimized counterexamples, and waveforms, then require both regression suites to pass after the fix. The deliverable includes a threat model, pinned versions, one-command reproduction, failure cases, the fix diff, and a defensive postmortem. Acceptance does not reward a stealthier vulnerability: both independent paths must detect the old defect, reject a bad fix, and explain residual risk within the authorization boundary.
