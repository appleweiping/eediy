## This is not another modulation survey; it moves from distance to modern decoding

MIT 6.451 **Principles of Digital Communication II, Spring 2005** is David Forney's graduate course and the second term after 6.450. The official [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/pages/syllabus/) makes 6.450 a strict prerequisite; in the original class, anyone without it had to justify equivalent preparation in an interview. The course starts with constellations, coding gain, and hard versus soft decisions on AWGN channels. It then reaches linear block codes, finite fields, Reed–Solomon and convolutional codes, trellises, codes on graphs, sum-product, turbo/LDPC/RA codes, lattice and trellis codes, and finally linear Gaussian channels. The spine is not a catalogue of code names. It asks how geometric or algebraic structure changes attainable error rates and how a decoder exploits that structure.

Spring 2005 is the course version, not a vague date attached to recently recorded videos. OCW preserves that offering's notes, recordings, problems, and answers, but supplies no outside TA, homework grading, MIT credit, or certificate. The class met twice a week for 1.5 hours and was a 12-credit H-level subject. Those figures describe the campus workload; they do not imply that watching 3 hours of video completes a week.

## Pass three threshold problems or return to 6.450

Problem 1: for BPSK or a small QAM constellation, derive a log-likelihood ratio from the conditional density, state what a hard decision discards relative to a soft decision, and obtain a high-SNR approximation to the uncoded error probability. Problem 2: perform addition, multiplication, and inversion in a small finite field, construct a parity-check matrix from a generator matrix, and interpret the syndrome. Problem 3: draw the trellis for a short convolutional code and hand-compute Viterbi path metrics, or write one sum-product message update on a tiny factor graph.

At least 2 of the 3 must be completed independently before Lecture 1. If the first response only quotes a Q-function, the second confuses modulo-2 arithmetic with a general finite field, or the third cannot distinguish a state from a path metric, review probability, linear algebra, and 6.450 first. This placement diagnostic is an EEDIY editorial gate, not an MIT prerequisite waiver.

## Read the 25 lectures as three changes of language

The official [calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/pages/calendar/) lists Lectures 1–25. Lectures 1–4 establish a geometric language through Euclidean distance, AWGN capacity, small constellations, and hard or soft decoding. Lectures 5–12 switch to the algebraic language of binary block codes, finite fields, and Reed–Solomon codes. Lectures 13–21 switch again to the algorithmic language of convolutional codes, trellises, graphs, sum-product, and turbo/LDPC/RA codes. Lectures 22–25 treat lattice and trellis codes and linear Gaussian channels. At every change, maintain an “object–metric–decoder” sheet so that derivations from one chapter do not remain isolated.

The official [video index](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/video_galleries/video-lectures/) supplies all 25 recordings with downloadable notes, while [readings and lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/pages/readings-and-lecture-notes/) includes a complete course-notes PDF. Read definitions and the destination of a derivation before watching, use the recording for the turning points in the argument, and then reconstruct the proof outline with the sources closed. Passive viewing conceals the two major abstraction jumps—from fields to codes and from codes to graph algorithms.

## Lock first attempts on all 10 problem sets before opening solutions

The official [assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/pages/assignments/) page publishes Problem Sets 1–10 with a solution for every set. The 2005 syllabus says the last set was not collected but was still expected work; in the original class, solutions appeared after the due-date lecture and submissions received 0, 1, or 2. Independent study should preserve that delay. Lock a first attempt, assumptions, and failure points before opening an answer, or recognition of a solution will replace model selection, bound choice, and decoder construction.

Tag each correction as channel or model, distance or bound, algebra or field, trellis or graph construction, algorithm update, or asymptotic interpretation. A repeat must change the constellation, code parameters, or received sequence instead of copying the same algebra. Return to 6.450 if Problems 1–2 require a solution before a model can even be written. Pause at Problem Set 5 if code structure still cannot be related to minimum distance or syndromes; accumulating terminology will not close that gap.

## Use six exam papers for transfer, not repeated exposure

The official [exams](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/pages/exams/) page publishes the 2002, 2003, and 2005 midterms and finals, with solutions for all 6 papers. The original 2005 weights were approximately 35% midterm, 50% final, and 15% problem sets. The midterm allowed 3 pages of notes and the 3-hour final allowed 5. Those rules belong to the historical campus class and do not generate an MIT score for an outside learner.

Use the 2002 midterm as a first-half diagnostic, the 2003 midterm as a parallel form after correction, and reserve the 2005 paper until Lecture 12. Attempt only 2 finals in depth and keep the third genuinely unseen. Score with the official solution and a prewritten partial-credit rubric, explicitly marking steps that cannot be judged. Reciting Reed–Solomon structure while being unable to select a decoding path for a new received word is not transfer.

## EEDIY adds one reproducible decoder comparison, not a fictional MIT lab

The reviewed public course has no official hardware lab, code starter, or autograder. The EEDIY supplement is a seeded simulation notebook comparing uncoded BPSK, one short block or convolutional code, and hard/soft or Viterbi decoding through BER versus \(E_b/N_0\). Calibrate noise variance against the analytical uncoded BPSK curve, then report confidence intervals, sample counts, code-rate normalization, and the execution environment. This is an EEDIY supplement, not an official MIT 6.451 assignment, lab, or completion claim.

Do not reduce the notebook to one black-box communications-library call. Keep encoder, channel, metric, decoder, and estimator as 5 separately testable layers. Include zero-noise, extreme-noise, known-short-sequence, and analytical-baseline tests. A Monte Carlo curve judged only by a plausible shape can silently mix \(E_s/N_0\) with \(E_b/N_0\), or two-sided spectral density with per-dimension noise variance.

## The exit condition is an auditable communications argument

The completion dossier should contain the 3 threshold problems; an object–metric–decoder index for all 25 lectures; first attempts and an error taxonomy for Problem Sets 1–10; timed records for at least 1 midterm and 1 final; a formula sheet; and tests plus a BER report for the EEDIY notebook. For every conclusion, identify the channel model, the origin of the performance metric, decoder complexity, and the regime in which an approximation holds.

Do not mark the course complete if Euclidean distance cannot explain coding gain, generator/parity-check/trellis/graph descriptions cannot be related, or sum-product messages have no interpretable meaning on the graph.
