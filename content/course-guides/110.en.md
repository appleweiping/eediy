## Access and version notes

Cornell [ECE 4880 Radio Frequency Systems](https://ocw.ece.cornell.edu/courses/ece-4880-radio-frequency-systems) fits learners with RF foundations who want component nonidealities carried into a system budget. It moves from transmission lines and Smith charts to gain, noise, mismatch, nonlinearity, mixing, receiver architectures, and phase noise. The catch is substantial: its [lecture index](https://ocw.ece.cornell.edu/courses/ece-4880-radio-frequency-systems/ece-4880-lectures-notes-and-handouts) contains only Lectures 6–11 and Handouts 1–9, explicitly omitting Lectures 1–5. The formal prerequisite is ECE 3030 or instructor consent. “Begin at Lecture 6” is not a solution to that gap.

Someone who already knows transmission lines, S-parameters, and a basic RF budget will enjoy seeing component nonidealities propagate into system performance. A first-time RF learner should use the [syllabus](https://ocw.ece.cornell.edu/courses/ece-4880-radio-frequency-systems/ece-4880-syllabus/) to restore reference impedance and planes, S-parameters, and Smith matching first. RFIC bias and layout also sit outside this course; six surviving later lectures cannot double as an RFIC design class.

## The answer supply is generous, but the years are easy to misread

[Homework and exams](https://ocw.ece.cornell.edu/courses/ece-4880-radio-frequency-systems/ece-4880-homework-and-exams) provides solutions for HW1–HW10 and solved Prelims 1 and 2, an unusually useful public RF problem set. Calculate the entire cascade before opening a key, then hunt stage by stage for dB/dBm/W/Vrms, reference direction, noise reference plane, or ordering errors.

The final and its solution are labeled Fall 2015, while the main archive is Fall 2016. The older paper can test durable concepts but cannot reconstruct 2016 coverage or grading. RF work is especially vulnerable to a software screenshot swallowing units and reference planes; writing the gain, noise, and mismatch of every stage often catches more than a more elaborate simulation.

## Safety

The [projects and labs](https://ocw.ece.cornell.edu/courses/ece-4880-radio-frequency-systems/ece-4880-projects-and-labs) page has 6 unsolved PDFs covering instrumentation and spectrum, VNA/S-parameters/de-embedding, splitters and couplers, amplifiers, oscillators and mixers, and a free proposal. Later material mentions 600 MHz–2 GHz, roughly 30 dBm, and a jammer example. That belongs to a supervised compliant laboratory; it grants no authority for open-space operation.

Physical work requires authorized spectrum, calibrated instruments, qualified supervision, shielding or a dummy load and attenuation, and a stated power limit. [FCC Part 15](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-15) applies only within its jurisdiction; elsewhere, local rules govern. Without that setting, data, simulation, and controlled low-energy tests still teach the receiver budget.

## Outside the lab, build a receiver budget with four separate layers

Begin with S2P data as defined by the [IBIS Touchstone 2.1 specification](https://ibis.org/touchstone_ver2.1/touchstone_ver2_1.pdf), checking passive insertion and mismatch, reciprocity, passivity, and energy balance with port order, reference impedance, and reference plane stated. Touchstone can also contain active small-signal parameters and optional two-port noise parameters, so “Touchstone means passive” is itself a misconception.

The active layer needs bias, temperature, and reference plane, with gain derived from \(S_{21}\), transducer gain, or available gain and stability checked. Noise figure and a Friis cascade require noise parameters or a separate noise model. For nonlinearity, run a two-tone sweep with tone spacing and input range specified, extrapolating IIP3 only before compression where fundamental and IM3 slopes remain near one and three. A linear S2P cannot invent intermodulation. The LO layer then uses an independent phase-noise PSD with its single-sideband convention, carrier, and offset integration band to calculate integrated phase/time jitter and reciprocal mixing.

Sweep blocker level, bandwidth, and stage order using synthetic or clearly licensed data, and call the result a **simulation-only receiver budget**. It is not VNA calibration or Cornell Lab 6. If the four models each have a defensible source and range, and the analysis can explain which stage limits the receiver first, the incomplete archive still teaches excellent RF-system judgment.
