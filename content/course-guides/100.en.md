## Expand outward from the bit interface

The central object in 6.450 is not one modulation format; it is the bit-stream interface between source and channel. First draw an accountable chain: source distribution → source code/quantizer → waveform and signal space → noisy channel → sufficient statistic → detector/decoder. For every arrow, name the input object, output object, loss criterion, and assumption. If entropy, energy, probability of error, and bandwidth appear in one undifferentiated column, review probability, linear systems, and vector spaces first.

MIT's [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-450-principles-of-digital-communications-i-fall-2006/pages/syllabus/) identifies 6.450 as a Fall 2006 graduate introduction. The prerequisite is 6.011, with good undergraduate probability and linear-systems background expected. It explicitly calls this material the baseline for 6.451, 6.452, and 6.441. The chain audit above is an EEDIY placement exercise, not an official MIT opening quiz.

## The 2006 course contains 3 historical layers

The classroom calendar has 25 sessions. Sessions 1–7 move from a layered view, discrete source encoding, entropy, Markov sources, and Lempel–Ziv into quantization and waveform encoding. Sessions 8–12 cover measure, Fourier and sampling, degrees of freedom, signal space, Nyquist, PAM, and QAM. Sessions 13–19 cover random processes, Gaussian/WGN models, filtering, detection, the theorem of irrelevance, and baseband. Sessions 20–24 treat wireless propagation, Doppler and time spread, coherence, discrete-time baseband fading, RAKE, and CDMA; Session 25 is review.

The [Lecture Notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-450-principles-of-digital-communications-i-fall-2006/pages/lecture-notes/) are not 25 sets of session slides. They form a public manuscript: preface and contents, Chapters 1–9, and bibliography. The 9 chapters are digital communication; discrete source coding; quantization; source and channel waveforms; vector and signal space; channels, modulation, and demodulation; random processes and noise; detection, coding, and decoding; and wireless digital communication. The syllabus calls this the only required text.

The [Video Lectures](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-450-principles-of-digital-communications-i-fall-2006/video_galleries/video-lectures/) explicitly come from Fall 2003, not the 2006 classroom. The public sequence is Lectures 1–24, with no separate Lecture 25 review video. A study ledger should preserve the provenance “2006 syllabus/manuscript + 2003 videos” and resolve numbering or wording differences against the corresponding year.

## Test the order through 4 interfaces

Interface A is source→bits. Derive code length from the prefix-free condition, Kraft inequality, entropy bound, and AEP rather than only running Huffman; quantization also needs a distortion measure and rate. Interface B is bits→waveforms. Use an orthonormal basis to turn continuous waveforms into signal-space vectors, distinguish energy, dimension, bandwidth, and symbol interval, and derive the Nyquist pulse condition from zero ISI.

Interface C is waveform→decision. Derive binary and M-ary detectors from sufficient statistics and Euclidean distance under WGN, then explain why a matched filter retains decision-relevant information. Every BER curve must state \(E_b/N_0\), priors, and mapping. Interface D is decision→wireless channel. Pair Doppler/time spread with coherence time/frequency, distinguish flat or frequency-selective and coherent or incoherent detection, and state which layer a RAKE receiver or CDMA construction addresses.

Progress means adjacent interfaces close, not that videos played: source rate enters waveform rate, waveform enters a channel model, channel statistics enter a detector, and the detector metric creates an interpretable error event. Retain at least 1 counterexample per stage—for example, prefix codes are not unique, bandlimited does not mean time-limited, minimum-distance claims depend on a noise model, and Rayleigh fading cannot reuse an AWGN formula unchanged.

## 6.02, 6.450, and 6.451 solve different tasks

MIT [6.02 Digital Communication Systems](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-02-introduction-to-eecs-ii-digital-communication-systems-fall-2012/) is an undergraduate EECS introduction connecting 3 layers—bits, signals, and packets—through compression and error correction, physical channels, and networks. Its prerequisites are 18.03 and 6.01; problem sets require Python at the 6.00/6.01 level and include programming tasks and some real-channel experiments. Choose 6.02 when you need an introductory course that connects coding, physical links, and packet networks.

6.450 does not follow the packet-routing spine. It builds a pre-research mathematical frame for source coding, quantization, signal-space geometry, random processes, optimal detection, and wireless baseband models. MIT [6.451 Principles of Digital Communication II](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-451-principles-of-digital-communication-ii-spring-2005/) strictly lists 6.450 as prerequisite and shifts to approaching the Shannon limit: linear, block, and convolutional codes; Viterbi and trellises; turbo and LDPC; sum-product and BCJR; lattice and coded modulation. Choose 6.02 if your foundation is shaky, 6.450 for the rigorous source-to-detector chain, and 6.451 to pursue coding gain and modern codes.

## Four decision problems determine readiness for the second course

Decision problem 1: for a memoryless source, construct a prefix code, compare expected length with entropy, and explain how block extension changes redundancy. Decision problem 2: given symbol period and channel bandwidth, derive a zero-ISI pulse criterion and explain noise variance after matched filtering.

Decision problem 3: draw decision regions for 3 signal-space points in WGN and derive the structure of pairwise error and a union bound. Decision problem 4: given delay and Doppler parameters plus symbol duration, classify the channel's coherence regime and choose coherent, incoherent, or diversity/RAKE reasoning. Failure on item 1 returns to Chapters 2–3, item 2 to Chapters 4–6, item 3 to Chapters 7–8, and item 4 to Chapter 9. Complete at least 3 without solutions and check them by a second method before attempting 6.451.

## An unofficial Monte Carlo study only fills the missing experiment interface

Public 6.450 has no software requirement, code repository, or laboratory. EEDIY may add 1 baseband simulation explicitly labeled non-official: implement BPSK/QPSK or a small QAM constellation, add AWGN, and compare analytic with Monte Carlo BER; then add 1 flat-Rayleigh case and report confidence interval, random seed, sample count, normalization, and stopping criterion. Its purpose is to test detector and channel assumptions, not to generate a smooth-looking curve.

With pulse shaping, record roll-off, samples per symbol, filter delay, matched-filter sampling instant, and transient discard. With fading, distinguish perfect, estimated, or absent CSI. Do not “prove” very low BER from too few samples, and do not call a baseband simulation RF transmission, an MIT lab, or a hardware result. Python/NumPy, Julia, or MATLAB/Octave is acceptable, with versions and units frozen.
