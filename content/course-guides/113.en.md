## 6.661 puts sensitivity, aperture, and resolution in one budget

MIT [6.661 Spring 2003](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-661-receivers-antennas-and-signals-spring-2003/)
moves from thermal/shot noise, receiver sensitivity, mixers, and optical
detection into antennas/apertures, binary communication/coding,
interferometry, radar/SAR, and estimation. The
[calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-661-receivers-antennas-and-signals-spring-2003/pages/calendar/)
lists 24 lectures and 2 quizzes. It fits learners with probability/random
processes, Fourier analysis, linear systems, complex envelopes, and basic
antenna concepts who want to reason about detection and resolution together. It is
not a communication or antenna introduction when those foundations are weak.

## Course structure

Read the [lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-661-receivers-antennas-and-signals-spring-2003/pages/lecture-notes/)
through a `source → receiver input` link budget, a `receiver input →
statistic` noise/detection budget, and an `aperture/sampling → estimate`
resolution budget. Every noise model states one-/two-sided PSD, impedance,
bandwidth, and reference point. Recheck Friis through power flux, effective
aperture, and mismatch. In radar/SAR, reuse receiver noise and aperture to calculate range,
angular resolution, ambiguity, and estimation error.

## The 13 Solved Sets Are the Main Advantage

The [assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-661-receivers-antennas-and-signals-spring-2003/pages/assignments/)
publish Problem Set 1–13 with 13 solutions, plus hints for PS1. Write down the
model and first equation before reading a key, then correct missing
assumptions, normalization, and system interfaces. Regroup work into
noise/receivers, antennas/apertures, communication detection, and
imaging/radar/estimation. For a random problem, explain source, noise entry,
observable, estimator, and metric within 5 minutes rather than reciting
algebra.

## With no quiz papers, add one independent numerical exercise

The [syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-661-receivers-antennas-and-signals-spring-2003/pages/syllabus/)
and notes support theory study, but there are no public videos, code
repository, lab instructions, dataset, or quiz papers. Build an independent
numerical exercise from transmit power, propagation loss, aperture, noise
temperature, and bandwidth; it is not an official MIT assignment. Derive
detectable range and use Monte Carlo for
detection/false-alarm behavior, and vary aperture sampling to observe
resolution and sidelobes. Preserve seed, units, normalization, and confidence
interval. Turning off noise, enlarging aperture, and increasing trials are 3
sanity checks that must follow analytic trends. The project validates the 3
budgets; it is not an MIT hardware lab. Use one parameter table across them so
transmit power, bandwidth, aperture, and noise temperature keep the same
meaning. In the receiver model, locate the stage where noise enters and refer
it to the decision point; for the antenna, connect polarization, matching, and
effective aperture; for imaging, separate sampling sidelobes from estimator
error. If a curve disagrees with theory, check one-sided versus two-sided PSD,
power units, and reference planes before expanding the model. A useful oral
test is to explain how one parameter changes sensitivity, detection
probability, and spatial resolution in the three calculations.

The distinctive idea in 6.661 is that aperture, bandwidth, and noise
temperature jointly change receiver sensitivity, detection probability, and spatial
resolution together. The three subjects cannot be analyzed in isolation.
