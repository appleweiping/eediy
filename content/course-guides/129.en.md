## Classify it first: 6.777J is a MEMS design course, not public cleanroom training

MIT 6.777J / 2.372J Design and Fabrication of Microelectromechanical Devices was taught in Spring 2007 by Carol Livermore and Joel Voldman. The [official syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-777j-design-and-fabrication-of-microelectromechanical-devices-spring-2007/pages/syllabus/) describes a graduate course meeting 2 times per week for 1.5 hours. The same syllabus says that prior microfabrication experience is desirable; its public spine consists of device design, lumped modeling, transduction, electronic readout, packaging, and device-level tradeoffs.

“Fabrication” in the title is easy to misread. The public material requires understanding surface and bulk micromachining, materials, and process constraints, and every design must account for its process flow. OCW does not provide cleanroom authorization, equipment booking, local training, process recipes, a PDK, mask submission, device fabrication, or testing service. An independent route can reproduce the analysis and design evidence. It cannot call a CAD image or process diagram a fabricated device, and it must not relocate hazardous processing to a home.

## Entry diagnostic: close three gaps before drawing a structure

Complete three paper or numerical diagnostics before Lecture 1:

1. For a parallel-plate electrostatic actuator, derive electrical energy, electrostatic force, and mechanical restoring force. Check units and state when the equilibrium loses stability. If this is only formula substitution, review electrostatics, energy methods, and one-degree-of-freedom dynamics.
2. Draw the free-body diagram of a cantilever, estimate stiffness and first resonance, and derive their scaling with size. If changing length cannot be connected to stiffness, mass, and damping, review mechanics of materials and vibration.
3. Read a simplified process cross-section and infer ordering constraints among deposition, lithography, etching, sacrificial layers, and release. List where residual stress, stiction, undercut, and packaging could defeat the design. This is process reasoning, not a physical chemistry experiment.

Electronics cannot remain a blank box. At minimum, connect a capacitive or resistive sensor change to a small-signal readout model, refer noise back to the input, and identify which bandwidth, stability, or dynamic-range constraint feedback changes. Use the diagnostics to open `field-mechanics`, `process`, and `readout` review branches. They need not all be completed in advance, but their assumptions must become explicit in later model cards.

## Twenty-five lectures follow one device evidence chain

The [official calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-777j-design-and-fabrication-of-microelectromechanical-devices-spring-2007/pages/calendar/) gives the order of 25 lectures, while the [lecture-notes page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-777j-design-and-fabrication-of-microelectromechanical-devices-spring-2007/pages/lecture-notes/) exposes the corresponding notes. Maintain one fixed chain while studying: `specification budget → process cross-section → transducer/device model → electronics/noise/feedback → package/test`. Every lecture should change an identifiable link in that chain rather than produce one of 25 disconnected summaries.

### Lectures 1–5: language, process, and material constraints

Lectures 1–5 establish the MEMS introduction, fabrication, and material foundations. Their deliverable is not a process-name glossary. Produce a cross-section with dimensions, materials, structural and sacrificial layers, release direction, and critical tolerances. Every claim that a geometry is fabricable must also address linewidth, alignment, sidewall, residual stress, and whether the released structure still supports the intended function. If the public notes do not give a facility-specific design rule, mark it for confirmation with the target process rather than inventing a universal number.

Keep at least three columns in every scaling analysis: how geometry changes with scale, how the desired signal changes, and how parasitics and failure modes change. Capacitive gain, beam stiffness, thermal time constant, and fluid drag do not scale with one common exponent. Miniaturization is understood only when those exponents are connected back to the device specification.

### Lectures 6–18: mechanics, electronics, transduction, and dynamic models

Lectures 6 and 7 each run two foundation tracks in parallel: Lecture 6 is elasticity or electronics I, and Lecture 7 is structures or electronics II. Students chose a track according to their existing undergraduate mechanics or electronics preparation. Lecture 8 introduces lumped modeling, Lecture 9 transducers, Lecture 10 nonlinear dynamics, and Lecture 11 structures. Lecture 12 covers thermal systems and dissipation, Lecture 13 dissipative modeling, Lectures 14–15 fluids, Lecture 16 transport, Lecture 17 feedback, and Lecture 18 noise.

Maintain paired models for the same device in this segment: a lumped model simple enough for hand calculation and parameter sweeps, and a higher-fidelity reference that reveals when lumped assumptions fail. Each model records states, port variables, stored energy, dissipation, actuation, sensing, linearization point, and validity range. A simulation needs units, mesh or time step, tolerance, and at least one analytic limit; a finite-element color map cannot substitute for boundary conditions and convergence evidence.

From Lecture 17 onward, put control and noise back into the physical device instead of opening a detached “controller” chapter. When feedback gain rises, track stability margin, bandwidth, saturation, and noise shaping. A minimum detectable signal must be accompanied by noise spectral density, integration bandwidth, and the point to which noise is referred. A lone sensitivity number without those three items is not comparable.

### Lectures 19–25: packaging, tradeoffs, and four system classes

Lecture 19 treats packaging, Lecture 20 an in-class design, and Lecture 21 design tradeoffs. Lectures 22–25 examine power MEMS, optical MEMS, a capacitive accelerometer, and bioMEMS. Packaging must not be postponed until “after the device works”: interconnects, parasitic capacitance, cavity pressure, heat paths, fluid ports, optical windows, contamination, and calibration can all change the device geometry.

Create one tradeoff map for each of the final 4 cases: performance metric, dominant physics, fabrication constraint, readout or actuation, package, test method, and leading failure mode. The comparison is not a search for the best MEMS. It shows how the same modeling tools receive different weights in power, optical, inertial, and biological systems.

## Seven public problem sets still do not form a complete grading loop

The [assignments page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-777j-design-and-fabrication-of-microelectromechanical-devices-spring-2007/pages/assignments/) exposes Problem Sets 1–7. Solutions are public for Problem Sets 1–4, 6, and 7; no solution is posted for Problem Set 5. It also provides a literature “Treasure Hunt” prompt, sample, and worksheet, plus the MATLAB files `prob6_10.m` and `prob6_11.m` supporting Problem Set 4. This is enough for a useful feedback loop, but the page provides no login grader, staff annotations, or official score.

Attempt every set under a time limit before opening its solution, then write a correction memo that classifies errors by physical assumption, equation, unit, order of magnitude, numerical implementation, or missing argument. Preserve Problem Set 5 as having no official answer: support it with an independent derivation, another text, limiting cases, and code tests rather than presenting a self-authored result as an official solution. The 2007 `.m` files are not guaranteed to run unchanged in a current MATLAB or Octave release. Record interpreter and version, the original error, and the smallest compatibility diff; porting is an EEDIY supplement.

In the original course, 7 individual homework sets accounted for 15%, a take-home design problem for 35%, and the final project for 50%. The design problem appears in the assessment plan and calendar, but its prompt is absent from the public assignments page. An independent learner therefore cannot claim to have completed the same assessment. A self-authored design review may partly fill the training gap, but it must be labeled an unofficial substitute and cannot inherit the original weight to manufacture an equivalent grade.

## The team project is valuable for stage reviews, not an invented tape-out

The [official project page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-777j-design-and-fabrication-of-microelectromechanical-devices-spring-2007/pages/projects/) specifies teams of 4–5. At Lecture 17, a 1–2-page preliminary report describes challenges, approach, tasks, and timeline. At Lecture 22, a 2–5-page interim report covers progress, the plan for finishing, and outstanding issues. A 30-minute presentation per team is scheduled 5 days after Lecture 25. The target final-report length is 25–30 pages; shorter is acceptable and longer is discouraged. The original course also required paper and electronic copies. Half of an individual project grade came from the team grade and half from peer grading, an instructor and peer environment that an ordinary independent learner cannot reproduce.

Example directions include a fast DEP cell sorter above 1000 cells/s, a piezoresistive biomolecular sensor, an RF-IF filter, and a micro flex-tester. Each closes a chain across a microfabricated device, drive or detection electronics, and a packaging concept rather than submitting an isolated mechanical structure. The public page supplies briefs and reporting guidance, not complete reference designs, manufacturing files, devices, experimental data, or answers.

An independent project can preserve 4 gates: specification and literature audit; cross-section and first-order model; joint electronics, noise, and package review; parameter sweep and frozen test plan. Without institutional facilities, the final result is a design study. Public material properties may support sensitivity analysis and alternative process assumptions, but the result must not say “fabricated” or “measured.” The test plan distinguishes what simulation verifies, what needs a bench device, and what becomes measurable only after cleanroom and packaging work.

## Cleanroom boundary: the home route stops at models, literature, and process planning

The [OSHA semiconductor device fabrication overview](https://www.osha.gov/semiconductors/silicon/device-fabrication) identifies hazards including wet-etch acids such as HF, HCl, and H2SO4, reactive gases, RF radiation, caustics, solvents, and toxic residues, together with ventilation, isolation, engineering controls, monitoring, and training. No chemical recipe, equipment setting, or operating sequence belongs here. HF, piranha, solvent processing, vacuum systems, plasma, reactive gases, and high-temperature furnaces are not home DIY projects.

Physical fabrication belongs only in an authorized institutional facility after its responsible staff provide equipment- and process-specific training, risk assessment, and supervision. Reading the online course does not grant access. Even handling fabricated dies can introduce sharp edges, particles, packaging materials, wire-bonding equipment, and test voltages that belong in a local safety process. Without those conditions, stop at cross-sections, conceptual CAD, lumped or finite-element models, readout models, noise budgets, and test plans; those can still constitute serious design evidence.

## The boundary with 6.152J: design modeling or institutional process laboratory

[MIT 6.152J Micro/Nano Processing Technology](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-152j-micro-nano-processing-technology-fall-2005/) centers diffusion, oxidation, lithography, CVD, and team laboratory fabrication and testing. 6.777J better fits a learner seeking a loop from specifications through device physics, dynamics, electronic interfaces, packaging, and system tradeoffs. 6.152J better fits someone who already has compliant access to a micro/nanofabrication facility and needs process practice.

The courses are complementary, but 6.152J laboratories cannot be translated into home chemistry, and a 6.777J paper design is not fabrication experience. With institutional access, use 6.777J for the design review and let facility staff decide what can enter a controlled process. Without that access, the simulation-only 6.777J route is the honest choice.

## An auditable definition of completion

Using the official Lecture, Problem Set, and project numbering above, an EEDIY-recommended record contains 3 prerequisite diagnostics, a specification-chain index for Lectures 1–25, first attempts and correction memos for Problem Sets 1–7, the Treasure Hunt literature audit, a separate evidence packet for the unsolved Problem Set 5, and a design study that passes all 4 gates. Its project package contains a specification budget, dimensioned and material-labeled cross-section, model assumptions, parameter sweeps, electronics and noise budgets, package interfaces, failure modes, and a tiered test plan.

Finish with a public-boundary table: which notes, prompts, and solutions came from OCW; which design problem, grader, annotations, peer grade, device, and experimental data were unavailable; and which script migrations, validation tests, and self-authored project elements came from EEDIY. The evidence can support completion of independent design training on public materials. It cannot support an MIT grade, cleanroom qualification, or fabrication résumé.
