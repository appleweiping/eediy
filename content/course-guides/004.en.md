## 18.06SC pairs Strang with enough problems to learn from mistakes

Choose MIT [18.06SC](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/) when a first linear-algebra course needs problems and solutions as well as Strang's explanations. The [Resource Index](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/pages/resource-index/) describes the real course better than a playlist. The sequence moves from elimination and the four fundamental subspaces through least squares, eigenvalues, positive-definite matrices, and SVD. Although the catalogue lists 18.02, the course description says calculus is not needed to complete the material; matrix arithmetic and vector geometry are the practical prerequisites.

## Assignments and feedback

The public index contains 31 topic-level problem groups, all with solutions. Exam 1–3 and the [final exam](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/pages/final-exam/) also include answers. Attempt each group independently, then mark the precise cause of every error in a second color: confusing the nullspace with the left nullspace, for example, or failing to check that a least-squares residual is orthogonal to the column space. “Careless” is not a diagnosis that helps on the next matrix.

The most revealing exercise is a low-rank matrix examined completely: echelon form, bases and dimensions of all four subspaces, least-squares projection, eigenstructure, and SVD, with software checked against a hand-computable miniature. For runnable intuition, Eric Ayars's unofficial [Elementary Linear Algebra notebooks](https://github.com/ea42gh/linear-algebra-notebooks) are a useful companion. They are CC BY 4.0, contain more than 110 Jupyter notebooks, and use continuous integration; they are not MIT answers and should support rather than replace the native problems.

Prepare three matrices that make different abstractions visible: a rectangular rank-deficient matrix for the four subspaces and least squares, a symmetric positive-definite matrix for energy, eigendecomposition, and Cholesky, and a nearly singular matrix for condition number and roundoff. Hand-compute a small version of each before scaling it in software, then check \(A\mathbf x\), an orthogonal residual, or a reconstruction error. Printing eigenvalues without bases, projections, and residuals does not identify which structure has actually been understood.

In particular, compare the domains of eigendecomposition and SVD. For a rectangular or nonsymmetric matrix, state the conditions each factorization needs, the spaces containing its left and right vectors, and the reconstruction. Rescale a single column and observe the singular values, condition number, and least-squares solution. The example connects numerical linear algebra, signal subspaces, and low-rank approximation while preventing the vague claim that every factorization merely finds “principal directions.”

## Before moving on, distinguish three ideas

First, understand elimination as a way to expose subspace structure. Next, derive normal equations from orthogonal projection. Finally, explain which questions eigenvalues and singular values answer differently. The course has no official programming project, so there is no reason to invent an “MIT project.” For optional computation, try low-rank image approximation with matrix dimensions, a truncation rule, an error measure, and a small matrix that can be checked by hand.

Before leaving the course, take a mixed exam. If an unfamiliar matrix first prompts questions about rank, subspace, projection, or spectral structure—rather than a search for a familiar formula—the central habit of 18.06SC has transferred.
