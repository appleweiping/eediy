## First understand what this “self-study edition” actually contains

The recommended release is [MIT 18.06SC Linear Algebra, Fall 2011](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/), not a playlist extracted from a classroom course. MIT reorganized it as a Scholar course: for most study topics, the course brings together the relevant Strang reading, a lecture summary, lecture video, problems, and solutions; entries such as Overview and Exam Review do not carry that complete bundle. The course has three units. Unit 1 develops \(Ax=b\), elimination, independence, and the four fundamental subspaces; Unit 2 covers least squares, orthogonality, determinants, and eigenvalues; Unit 3 reaches positive definite matrices, similarity, singular value decomposition, and applications. Use the [Resource Index](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/pages/resource-index/) to determine what is genuinely present instead of inferring scope from video titles.

The [Syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/pages/syllabus/) preserves an easily misunderstood detail. MIT lists 18.02 Multivariable Calculus as the catalog prerequisite, yet the course description explicitly says calculus knowledge is not required to complete the material. The immediately useful preparation is comfort with vectors, matrices, and three-dimensional coordinates. Someone who can differentiate but cannot explain matrix multiplication is not ready merely because of the calculus course; someone who has not finished multivariable calculus need not postpone the course for that reason alone.

### Use the first native problem set as the readiness check

Do not substitute a generic linear-algebra placement quiz. Read the Lecture 1 summary and its textbook section, then attempt the first Problems item in the Resource Index without opening the solution. Show every row operation, verify the result by matrix multiplication, and explain the same \(Ax=b\) as a linear combination of columns. If the trouble is mostly notation, continue. If “solution of a system,” “vector in the column space,” and “eliminated matrix” still blur together, spend several days reviewing matrix arithmetic and vector geometry, then repeat the official problem. This uses the course’s own first task rather than an EEDIY-invented entrance exam.

## Use the 31 topic problem sets and 4 exams correctly

The public index contains 31 topic-level Problems files, and solutions are available for all 31. It also exposes Exam 1, Exam 2, Exam 3, and the Final Exam—4 exams in total—with solutions for each. That completeness is unusually valuable, but adjacent answers can also create false fluency. A sound cycle is to study the summary and video, close the material, solve the problems independently, correct them in a second color, and record one precise cause per error. “Confused the left nullspace with the nullspace” or “omitted \((A^TA)^{-1}\) in the projection” is useful; “careless” is not.

At the end of each unit, take its exam under a time limit. Reserve Exam 1, Exam 2, and Exam 3 for the ends of the three units, and keep the public [Final Exam](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/pages/final-exam/) for cumulative testing. Preserve the first script and attach a correction sheet that classifies each miss as modeling, theorem selection, algebra, or verification. Retaking only missed questions does not replace a second full paper, because much of linear algebra is recognizing the relevant structure among mixed problem types.

## A reading order that works without a class

In the first stage, treat elimination as a computational way to reveal subspace structure. Move through elimination, inverses, LU factorization, transposes, and permutations before column spaces, nullspaces, rank, and the four fundamental subspaces. The stage output should not be copied definitions. Construct one rank-deficient matrix and provide its echelon form, bases and dimensions for all four subspaces, and a check of the dimension relationships.

Begin the second stage with orthogonal vectors, projections, and least squares, then study Gram–Schmidt, determinants, eigenvalues, and eigenvectors in differential equations. Choose a small overdetermined system, compute the normal equations by hand, and verify that the residual is orthogonal to the column space. In the third stage, proceed to positive definite matrices, similar matrices, complex matrices, SVD, pseudoinverses, graphs, and linear transformations. By the end, explain rank, eigenstructure, and singular values for the same low-rank matrix rather than merely printing software output.

The official syllabus estimates about 150 hours for the entire course, with more than half spent on preparation and assignments. That is an estimate for completing this Scholar release, not a universal number of weeks for every learner. A video-only pass will feel much faster, but it discards the course’s scarce asset: a dense, fully solved chain of native problems.

## There is no official project, and one should not be invented

After checking the official Resource Index and course page, the public 2011 release contains no laboratory, programming assignment, or course project. Calling a new NumPy notebook an “MIT 18.06SC project” would blur provenance. A more honest and stronger completion record is the set of 31 independently attempted and corrected problem files, 4 timed exam scripts, one page of error categories for each unit, and three self-chosen examples that connect calculation back to geometry. Numerical software may check elimination, eigenvalues, or SVD, but label it as a personal supplement and retain the hand-first, software-second order.

If an application is desirable, start it after the course and label it explicitly as an unofficial extension, such as least-squares fitting or low-rank image approximation. State the matrix dimensions, error measure, singular-value truncation rule, and one hand-computable miniature example. Without those items, an attractive plot says little about command of the mathematics.

## Version, offline package, cost, and licensing boundaries

The current index includes a final lecture from 2023. It is a later addition, not evidence that the 2011 assessment sequence has become a 2023 offering. Reading assignments give section references for both the 4th and 5th editions of Strang. The textbook does not automatically become free because the OCW page is open; when using another edition, match topic names and sections rather than copying section numbers mechanically.

[Download Course](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/download/) is useful for preserving notes, problems, and solutions, but the package explicitly excludes audio and video; recordings must be accessed or downloaded separately. No hardware is required. Paper, a pencil, and a PDF-capable device are sufficient, while numerical software is optional. MIT-owned OCW content is governed by the license shown on the site, and textbooks, embedded media, and other third-party material may carry separate rights. Public readability is not permission to redistribute the whole collection as a new bundle.
